## Definitions and Examples

The integers mod $n$ and the symmetries of a triangle or a rectangle are examples of groups. A **binary operation** or **law of composition** on a set $G$ is a function $G \times G \rightarrow G$ that assigns to each pair $(a,b) \in G \times G$ a unique element $a \circ b$, or $ab$ in $G$, called the composition of $a$ and $b$. A **group** $(G, \circ )$ is a set $G$ together with a law of composition $(a,b) \mapsto a \circ b$ that satisfies the following axioms.

- The law of composition is **associative**. That is,
  $$(a \circ b) \circ c = a \circ (b \circ c)$$
  for $a, b, c \in G$.
- There exists an element $e \in G$, called the **identity element**, such that for any element $a \in G$
  $$e \circ a = a \circ e = a.$$
- For each element $a \in G$, there exists an **inverse element** in $G$, denoted by $a^{-1}$, such that
  $$a \circ a^{-1} = a^{-1} \circ a = e.$$

A group $G$ with the property that $a \circ b = b \circ a$ for all $a, b \in G$ is called **abelian** or **commutative**. Groups not satisfying this property are said to be **nonabelian** or **noncommutative**.

> *There is no notion of commutativity built into the language, so **abelian** is
> expressed where it is needed as an explicit hypothesis
> $\forall x, y;\ xy = yx$.*

**Example.** The integers ${\mathbb Z } = \{ \ldots , -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots \}$ form a group under the operation of addition. The binary operation on two integers $m, n \in {\mathbb Z}$ is just their sum. Since the integers under addition already have a well-established notation, we will use the operator $+$ instead of $\circ$; that is, we shall write $m + n$ instead of $m \circ n$. The identity is $0$, and the inverse of $n \in {\mathbb Z}$ is written as $-n$ instead of $n^{-1}$. Notice that the set of integers under addition have the additional property that $m + n = n + m$ and therefore form an abelian group.

Most of the time we will write $ab$ instead of $a \circ b$; however, if the group already has a natural operation such as addition in the integers, we will use that operation. That is, if we are adding two integers, we still write $m + n$, $-n$ for the inverse, and $0$ for the identity as usual. We also write $m - n$ instead of $m + (-n)$.

It is often convenient to describe a group in terms of an addition or multiplication table. Such a table is called a **Cayley table**.

**Example.** The integers mod $n$ form a group under addition modulo $n$. Consider ${\mathbb Z}_5$, consisting of the equivalence classes of the integers $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$. We define the group operation on ${\mathbb Z}_5$ by modular addition. We write the binary operation on the group additively; that is, we write $m + n$. The element $0$ is the identity of the group and each element in ${\mathbb Z}_5$ has an inverse. For instance, $2 + 3 = 3 + 2 = 0$. The table below is a Cayley table for ${\mathbb Z}_5$. By the proposition on equivalence classes in $\mathbb Z_n$, ${\mathbb Z}_n = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1 \}$ is a group under the binary operation of addition mod $n$.

Cayley table for $({\mathbb Z_5}, +)$:

$$\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
+ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\hline
0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
1 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 0 \\
2 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 1 \\
3 & 3 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\
4 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3
\end{array}$$

**Example.** Not every set with a binary operation is a group. For example, if we let modular multiplication be the binary operation on ${\mathbb Z}_n$, then ${\mathbb Z}_n$ fails to be a group. The element 1 acts as a group identity since $1 \cdot k = k \cdot 1 = k$ for any $k \in {\mathbb Z}_n$; however, a multiplicative inverse for $0$ does not exist since $0 \cdot k = k \cdot 0 = 0$ for every $k$ in ${\mathbb Z}_n$. Even if we consider the set ${\mathbb Z}_n \setminus \{0 \}$, we still may not have a group. For instance, let $2 \in {\mathbb Z}_6$. Then $2$ has no multiplicative inverse since

$$\begin{aligned}
0 \cdot 2 & = 0 \qquad 1 \cdot 2 = 2 \\
2 \cdot 2 & = 4 \qquad 3 \cdot 2 = 0 \\
4 \cdot 2 & = 2 \qquad 5 \cdot 2 = 4
\end{aligned}$$

By the proposition on equivalence classes in $\mathbb Z_n$, every nonzero $k$ does have an inverse in ${\mathbb Z}_n$ if $k$ is relatively prime to $n$. Denote the set of all such nonzero elements in ${\mathbb Z}_n$ by $U(n)$. Then $U(n)$ is a group called the **group of units** of ${\mathbb Z}_n$. The table below is a Cayley table for the group $U(8)$.

Multiplication table for $U(8)$:

$$\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\cdot & 1 & 3 & 5 & 7 \\
\hline
1       & 1 & 3 & 5 & 7 \\
3       & 3 & 1 & 7 & 5 \\
5       & 5 & 7 & 1 & 3 \\
7       & 7 & 5 & 3 & 1
\end{array}$$

**Example.** The symmetries of an equilateral triangle described in the section on symmetries form a nonabelian group. As we observed, it is not necessarily true that $\alpha \beta = \beta \alpha$ for two symmetries $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Using the Cayley table for this group, we can easily check that the symmetries of an equilateral triangle are indeed a group. We will denote this group by either $S_3$ or $D_3$, for reasons that will be explained later.

**Example.** We use ${\mathbb M}_2 ( {\mathbb R})$ to denote the set of all $2 \times 2$ matrices. Let $GL_2({\mathbb R})$ be the subset of ${\mathbb M}_2 ( {\mathbb R})$ consisting of invertible matrices; that is, a matrix

$$A =
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{pmatrix}$$

is in $GL_2( {\mathbb R})$ if there exists a matrix $A^{-1}$ such that $A A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = I$, where $I$ is the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix. For $A$ to have an inverse is equivalent to requiring that the determinant of $A$ be nonzero; that is, $\det A = ad - bc \neq 0$. The set of invertible matrices forms a group called the **general linear group**. The identity of the group is the identity matrix

$$I =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.$$

The inverse of $A \in GL_2( {\mathbb R})$ is

$$A^{-1} =
\frac{1}{ad-bc}
\begin{pmatrix}
d & -b \\
-c & a
\end{pmatrix}.$$

The product of two invertible matrices is again invertible. Matrix multiplication is associative, satisfying the other group axiom. For matrices it is not true in general that $AB = BA$; hence, $GL_2({\mathbb R})$ is another example of a nonabelian group.

**Example.** Let

$$\begin{aligned}
1 & =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0\\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\qquad
I
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1\\
-1 & 0
\end{pmatrix} \\
J & =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & i\\
i & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\qquad
K =
\begin{pmatrix}
i & 0\\
0 & -i
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}$$

where $i^2 = -1$. Then the relations $I^2 = J^2 = K^2 = -1$, $IJ=K$, $JK = I$, $KI = J$, $JI = -K$, $KJ = -I$, and $IK = -J$ hold. The set $Q_8 = \{\pm 1, \pm I, \pm J, \pm K  \}$ is a group called the **quaternion group**. Notice that $Q_8$ is noncommutative.

**Example.** Let ${\mathbb C}^\ast$ be the set of nonzero complex numbers. Under the operation of multiplication ${\mathbb C}^\ast$ forms a group. The identity is $1$. If $z = a+bi$ is a nonzero complex number, then

$$z^{-1} = \frac{a -bi}{a^2 +b^2}$$

is the inverse of $z$. It is easy to see that the remaining group axioms hold.

> *None of these seven examples can be stated formally here: each needs a
> concrete sort the abstract theory of a group does not provide — modular
> arithmetic for $\mathbb{Z}_5$ and $U(8)$, a finite tabulated operation for
> $S_3$, $2 \times 2$ real matrices and determinants for $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and
> $Q_8$, the complex numbers for $\mathbb{C}^\ast$. The theorems below hold in
> all of them at once.*

A group is **finite**, or has **finite order**, if it contains a finite number of elements; otherwise, the group is said to be **infinite** or to have **infinite order**. The **order** of a finite group is the number of elements that it contains. If $G$ is a group containing $n$ elements, we write $|G| = n$. The group ${\mathbb Z}_5$ is a finite group of order $5$; the integers ${\mathbb Z}$ form an infinite group under addition, and we sometimes write $|{\mathbb Z}| = \infty$.

### Basic Properties of Groups

The remainder of the section establishes, from the axioms alone, the facts every
group obeys.

## Proposition

> The identity element in a group $G$ is unique; that is, there exists only one element $e \in G$ such that $eg = ge = g$ for all $g \in G$.

**Proof.** If $e$ and $e'$ are both identities, then $e = ee' = e'$.

Equivalently, any left identity $x$ is $e$: applying $x$'s identity property at
$e$ gives $xe = e$, while the identity law gives $xe = x$, so $x = e$.

```2b4m
theorem identityUnique: forall x: G; (forall g: G; op(x, g) = g) -> x = e
proof
  @generalize-x |
    fix x: G {
      @given-x-is-a-left-identity |
        assume forall g: G; op(x, g) = g {
          @x-is-a-left-identity |
            forall g: G; op(x, g) = g
            [by hypothesis given-x-is-a-left-identity]
          @x-sends-the-identity-to-the-identity |
            op(x, e) = e
            [by apply_at(e) x-is-a-left-identity]
          @the-identity-sends-x-to-x |
            op(x, e) = x
            [using specialize identityRight(x)]
          @conclusion-x-is-the-identity |
            x = e
            [by rewrite the-identity-sends-x-to-x x-sends-the-identity-to-the-identity]
        }
      @a-left-identity-is-the-identity |
        (forall g: G; op(x, g) = g) -> x = e
        [by discharge given-x-is-a-left-identity]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall x: G; (forall g: G; op(x, g) = g) -> x = e
    [by generalize generalize-x]
qed
```

Inverses in a group are also unique. If $g'$ and $g''$ are both inverses of an element $g$ in a group $G$, then $gg' = g'g = e$ and $gg'' = g''g = e$. We want to show that $g' = g''$, but $g' = g'e = g'(gg'') = (g'g)g'' = eg'' = g''$. We summarize this fact in the following proposition.

## Proposition

> If $g$ is any element in a group $G$, then the inverse of $g$, denoted by $g^{-1}$, is unique.

Equivalently, a right inverse of $a$ is exactly $a^{-1}$.

```2b4m
theorem inverseUnique: forall a, b: G; op(a, b) = e -> b = inv(a)
proof
  @generalize-a |
    fix a: G {
      @generalize-b |
        fix b: G {
          @given-b-is-a-right-inverse |
            assume op(a, b) = e {
              @a-times-b-is-the-identity |
                op(a, b) = e
                [by hypothesis given-b-is-a-right-inverse]
              @the-identity-absorbs-on-the-left |
                op(e, b) = b
                [using specialize identityLeft(b)]
              @the-inverse-cancels-a |
                op(inv(a), a) = e
                [using specialize inverseLeft(a)]
              @reassociate |
                op(op(inv(a), a), b) = op(inv(a), op(a, b))
                [using assoc(associative)]
              @the-identity-absorbs-on-the-right |
                op(inv(a), e) = inv(a)
                [using specialize identityRight(inv(a))]
              // b = eb = (a⁻¹a)b = a⁻¹(ab) = a⁻¹e = a⁻¹
              @conclusion-b-is-the-inverse |
                b = inv(a)
                [using chain the-identity-absorbs-on-the-left the-inverse-cancels-a
                  reassociate a-times-b-is-the-identity the-identity-absorbs-on-the-right]
            }
          @a-right-inverse-is-the-inverse |
            op(a, b) = e -> b = inv(a)
            [by discharge given-b-is-a-right-inverse]
        }
      @discharge-b |
        forall b: G; op(a, b) = e -> b = inv(a)
        [by generalize generalize-b]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall a, b: G; op(a, b) = e -> b = inv(a)
    [by generalize generalize-a]
qed
```

## Proposition

> Let $G$ be a group. If $a, b \in G$, then $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$.

**Proof.** Let $a, b \in G$. Then $abb^{-1}a^{-1} = aea^{-1} = aa^{-1} = e$. Similarly, $b^{-1}a^{-1}ab = e$. But by the previous proposition, inverses are unique; hence, $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$.

That is, $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})$ re-associates to $a(bb^{-1})a^{-1}$, which
collapses to $e$; uniqueness of inverses then finishes it.

```2b4m
theorem invProduct: forall a, b: G; inv(op(a, b)) = op(inv(b), inv(a))
proof
  @generalize-a |
    fix a: G {
      @generalize-b |
        fix b: G {
          @rearrange |
            op(op(a, b), op(inv(b), inv(a))) = op(a, op(op(b, inv(b)), inv(a)))
            [using assoc(associative)]
          @b-invb-is-e |
            op(b, inv(b)) = e
            [using specialize inverseRight(b)]
          @e-inva |
            op(e, inv(a)) = inv(a)
            [using specialize identityLeft(inv(a))]
          @a-inva |
            op(a, inv(a)) = e
            [using specialize inverseRight(a)]
          // (ab)(b⁻¹a⁻¹) = a((bb⁻¹)a⁻¹) = a(e·a⁻¹) = a·a⁻¹ = e
          @the-product-is-the-identity |
            op(op(a, b), op(inv(b), inv(a))) = e
            [using chain rearrange b-invb-is-e e-inva a-inva]
          @the-swapped-inverses-are-the-inverse |
            op(inv(b), inv(a)) = inv(op(a, b))
            [using specialize inverseUnique(op(a, b), op(inv(b), inv(a))) the-product-is-the-identity]
          @conclusion-the-inverse-of-a-product |
            inv(op(a, b)) = op(inv(b), inv(a))
            [by symmetry the-swapped-inverses-are-the-inverse]
        }
      @discharge-b |
        forall b: G; inv(op(a, b)) = op(inv(b), inv(a))
        [by generalize generalize-b]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall a, b: G; inv(op(a, b)) = op(inv(b), inv(a))
    [by generalize generalize-a]
qed
```

## Proposition

> Let $G$ be a group. For any $a \in G$, $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$.

**Proof.** Observe that $a^{-1} (a^{-1})^{-1} = e$. Consequently, multiplying both sides of this equation by $a$, we have

$$(a^{-1})^{-1} = e (a^{-1})^{-1} = a a^{-1} (a^{-1})^{-1} = ae = a.$$

That is, since $a^{-1}a = e$, the element $a$ is a right inverse of $a^{-1}$, so
by uniqueness of inverses $a = (a^{-1})^{-1}$.

```2b4m
theorem invInvolution: forall a: G; inv(inv(a)) = a
proof
  @generalize-a |
    fix a: G {
      @inv-left |
        forall g: G; op(inv(g), g) = e
        [by cite inverseLeft]
      @inva-a-is-e |
        op(inv(a), a) = e
        [by apply_at(a) inv-left]
      @inv-unique |
        forall x, y: G; op(x, y) = e -> y = inv(x)
        [by cite inverseUnique]
      @inv-unique-at-inva-a |
        op(inv(a), a) = e -> a = inv(inv(a))
        [by apply_at(inv(a), a) inv-unique]
      @a-is-the-inverse-of-the-inverse |
        a = inv(inv(a))
        [by modus_ponens inv-unique-at-inva-a inva-a-is-e]
      @conclusion-the-inverse-is-involutive |
        inv(inv(a)) = a
        [by symmetry a-is-the-inverse-of-the-inverse]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall a: G; inv(inv(a)) = a
    [by generalize generalize-a]
qed
```

It makes sense to write equations with group elements and group operations. If $a$ and $b$ are two elements in a group $G$, does there exist an element $x \in G$ such that $ax = b$? If such an $x$ does exist, is it unique? The following proposition answers both of these questions positively.

## Proposition

> Let $G$ be a group and $a$ and $b$ be any two elements in $G$. Then the equations $ax = b$ and $xa = b$ have unique solutions in $G$.

**Proof.** Suppose that $ax = b$. We must show that such an $x$ exists. We can multiply both sides of $ax = b$ by $a^{-1}$ to find $x = ex = a^{-1}ax = a^{-1}b$.

To show uniqueness, suppose that $x_1$ and $x_2$ are both solutions of $ax = b$; then $ax_1 = b = ax_2$. So $x_1 = a^{-1}ax_1 = a^{-1}ax_2 = x_2$. The proof for the existence and uniqueness of the solution of $xa = b$ is similar.

Here is the $xa = b$ form. **Existence** — the solution is $x = ba^{-1}$, since
$(ba^{-1})a = b$:

```2b4m
theorem solutionExists: forall b, a: G; op(op(b, inv(a)), a) = b
proof
  @generalize-b |
    fix b: G {
      @generalize-a |
        fix a: G {
          @assoc-step |
            op(op(b, inv(a)), a) = op(b, op(inv(a), a))
            [using assoc(associative)]
          @inva-a |
            op(inv(a), a) = e
            [using specialize inverseLeft(a)]
          @b-times-identity-is-b |
            op(b, e) = b
            [using specialize identityRight(b)]
          // (b·a⁻¹)·a = b·(a⁻¹·a) = b·e = b
          @conclusion-the-solution-works |
            op(op(b, inv(a)), a) = b
            [using chain assoc-step inva-a b-times-identity-is-b]
        }
      @discharge-a |
        forall a: G; op(op(b, inv(a)), a) = b
        [by generalize generalize-a]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall b, a: G; op(op(b, inv(a)), a) = b
    [by generalize generalize-b]
qed
```

**Uniqueness** — any two solutions of $xa = b$ coincide, by right cancellation:

> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group.b4m:295:solutionUnique`.

```2b4m
theorem solutionUnique: forall x, a, b, y: G;
  op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
proof
  @generalize-x |
    fix x: G {
      @generalize-a |
        fix a: G {
          @generalize-b |
            fix b: G {
              @generalize-y |
                fix y: G {
                  @given-x |
                    assume op(x, a) = b {
                      @given-y |
                        assume op(y, a) = b {
                          @x-times-a-is-b |
                            op(x, a) = b
                            [by hypothesis given-x]
                          @y-times-a-is-b |
                            op(y, a) = b
                            [by hypothesis given-y]
                          // uniqueness = right-multiply by inv(a) and simplify
                          // each side via z·(a·a⁻¹) = z·e = z (the standard
                          // x = a⁻¹ax argument, here on the right).
                          @reassociate-x |
                            op(op(x, a), inv(a)) = op(x, op(a, inv(a)))
                            [using assoc(associative)]
                          @right-inv-cancels-a |
                            op(a, inv(a)) = e
                            [using specialize inverseRight(a)]
                          @identity-cancels-on-x |
                            op(x, e) = x
                            [using specialize identityRight(x)]
                          @reassociate-y |
                            op(op(y, a), inv(a)) = op(y, op(a, inv(a)))
                            [using assoc(associative)]
                          @identity-cancels-on-y |
                            op(y, e) = y
                            [using specialize identityRight(y)]
                          // x = op(x,e) = op(x,op(a,inva)) = op(op(x,a),inva) = op(op(y,a),inva) = op(y,op(a,inva)) = op(y,e) = y
                          @conclusion-x-is-y |
                            x = y
                            [using chain identity-cancels-on-x right-inv-cancels-a reassociate-x x-times-a-is-b y-times-a-is-b reassociate-y identity-cancels-on-y]
                        }
                      @disch-y |
                        op(y, a) = b -> x = y
                        [by discharge given-y]
                    }
                  @disch-x |
                    op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
                    [by discharge given-x]
                }
              @discharge-y |
                forall y: G; op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
                [by generalize generalize-y]
            }
          @discharge-b |
            forall b, y: G; op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
            [by generalize generalize-b]
        }
      @discharge-a |
        forall a, b, y: G; op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
        [by generalize generalize-a]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall x, a, b, y: G; op(x, a) = b -> op(y, a) = b -> x = y
    [by generalize generalize-x]
qed
```

## Proposition

> If $G$ is a group and $a, b, c \in G$, then $ba = ca$ implies $b = c$ and $ab = ac$ implies $b = c$.

This proposition tells us that the **right and left cancellation laws** are true in groups.

**Proof.** **Right cancellation** — right-multiply $ba = ca$ by $a^{-1}$ and
simplify each side via $b(aa^{-1}) = be = b$:

> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group.b4m:363:cancelRight`.

```2b4m
theorem cancelRight: forall b, a, c: G; op(b, a) = op(c, a) -> b = c
proof
  @generalize-b |
    fix b: G {
      @generalize-a |
        fix a: G {
          @generalize-c |
            fix c: G {
              @given-right-products-equal |
                assume op(b, a) = op(c, a) {
                  @right-products-equal |
                    op(b, a) = op(c, a)
                    [by hypothesis given-right-products-equal]
                  @reassociate-around-b |
                    op(op(b, a), inv(a)) = op(b, op(a, inv(a)))
                    [using assoc(associative)]
                  @a-inva |
                    forall g: G; op(g, inv(g)) = e
                    [by cite inverseRight]
                  @right-inv-cancels-a |
                    op(a, inv(a)) = e
                    [by apply_at(a) a-inva]
                  @id-right |
                    forall g: G; op(g, e) = g
                    [by cite identityRight]
                  @identity-cancels-on-b |
                    op(b, e) = b
                    [by apply_at(b) id-right]
                  @reassociate-around-c |
                    op(op(c, a), inv(a)) = op(c, op(a, inv(a)))
                    [using assoc(associative)]
                  @identity-cancels-on-c |
                    op(c, e) = c
                    [by apply_at(c) id-right]
                  // b = op(b,e) = op(b,op(a,inva)) = op(op(b,a),inva) = op(op(c,a),inva) = op(c,op(a,inva)) = op(c,e) = c
                  @conclusion-b-is-c |
                    b = c
                    [using chain identity-cancels-on-b right-inv-cancels-a reassociate-around-b right-products-equal reassociate-around-c identity-cancels-on-c]
                }
              @conclusion-the-implication |
                op(b, a) = op(c, a) -> b = c
                [by discharge given-right-products-equal]
            }
          @discharge-c |
            forall c: G; op(b, a) = op(c, a) -> b = c
            [by generalize generalize-c]
        }
      @discharge-a |
        forall a, c: G; op(b, a) = op(c, a) -> b = c
        [by generalize generalize-a]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall b, a, c: G; op(b, a) = op(c, a) -> b = c
    [by generalize generalize-b]
qed
```

**Left cancellation** is the mirror image: left-multiply by $a^{-1}$.

> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group.b4m:418:cancelLeft`.

```2b4m
theorem cancelLeft: forall a, b, c: G; op(a, b) = op(a, c) -> b = c
proof
  @generalize-a |
    fix a: G {
      @generalize-b |
        fix b: G {
          @generalize-c |
            fix c: G {
              @given-left-products-equal |
                assume op(a, b) = op(a, c) {
                  @left-products-equal |
                    op(a, b) = op(a, c)
                    [by hypothesis given-left-products-equal]
                  @reassociate-around-b |
                    op(op(inv(a), a), b) = op(inv(a), op(a, b))
                    [using assoc(associative)]
                  @inv-left |
                    forall g: G; op(inv(g), g) = e
                    [by cite inverseLeft]
                  @left-inv-cancels-a |
                    op(inv(a), a) = e
                    [by apply_at(a) inv-left]
                  @id-left |
                    forall g: G; op(e, g) = g
                    [by cite identityLeft]
                  @identity-cancels-on-b |
                    op(e, b) = b
                    [by apply_at(b) id-left]
                  @reassociate-around-c |
                    op(op(inv(a), a), c) = op(inv(a), op(a, c))
                    [using assoc(associative)]
                  @identity-cancels-on-c |
                    op(e, c) = c
                    [by apply_at(c) id-left]
                  // b = op(e,b) = op(op(inva,a),b) = op(inva,op(a,b)) = op(inva,op(a,c)) = op(op(inva,a),c) = op(e,c) = c
                  @conclusion-b-is-c |
                    b = c
                    [using chain identity-cancels-on-b left-inv-cancels-a reassociate-around-b left-products-equal reassociate-around-c identity-cancels-on-c]
                }
              @conclusion-the-implication |
                op(a, b) = op(a, c) -> b = c
                [by discharge given-left-products-equal]
            }
          @discharge-c |
            forall c: G; op(a, b) = op(a, c) -> b = c
            [by generalize generalize-c]
        }
      @discharge-b |
        forall b, c: G; op(a, b) = op(a, c) -> b = c
        [by generalize generalize-b]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall a, b, c: G; op(a, b) = op(a, c) -> b = c
    [by generalize generalize-a]
qed
```

We can use exponential notation for groups just as we do in ordinary algebra. If $G$ is a group and $g \in G$, then we define $g^0 = e$. For $n \in {\mathbb N}$, we define

$$g^n = \underbrace{g \cdot g \cdots g}_{n \; \text{times}}$$

and

$$g^{-n} = \underbrace{g^{-1} \cdot g^{-1} \cdots g^{-1}}_{n \; \text{times}}.$$

## Theorem — Laws of Exponents

> In a group, the usual laws of exponents hold; that is, for all $g, h \in G$,
>
> 1. $g^mg^n = g^{m+n}$ for all $m, n \in {\mathbb Z}$;
> 2. $(g^m)^n = g^{mn}$ for all $m, n \in {\mathbb Z}$;
> 3. $(gh)^n = (h^{-1}g^{-1})^{-n}$ for all $n \in {\mathbb Z}$. Furthermore, if $G$ is abelian, then $(gh)^n = g^nh^n$.

**Proof.** The exponent $g^n$ is defined by two-sided recursion — $g^0 = e$,
$g^{n+1} = g^n\cdot g$, and $g^{n-1} = g^n\cdot g^{-1}$, so a negative exponent
means repeated multiplication by the inverse — and each law goes by induction on
the exponent in both directions. Laws (1) and (2) and the general form of (3)
hold in any group; the second clause of (3), $(gh)^n = g^nh^n$, needs
commutativity.

> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group/power.b4m:96:powerAdd`.
>
> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group/power.b4m:271:powerMul`.
>
> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/group/power.b4m:651:powerProductAbelian`.

```2b4m
// (1)  g^m · g^n = g^{m+n}
theorem exponentLawProduct: forall g: G; forall m, n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: G {
      @generalize-m |
        fix m: Int {
          // base case n = ZERO.
          @power-zero |
            power(g, ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(g)]
          @op-with-identity |
            op(power(g, m), e) = power(g, m)
            [using specialize identityRight(power(g, m))]
          // op(g^m, g^0) = op(g^m, e) = g^m = g^(m+0).
          @base-lhs-collapses |
            op(power(g, m), power(g, ZERO)) = power(g, m)
            [using chain power-zero op-with-identity]
          @add-m-zero-flipped |
            m = add(m, ZERO)
            [using arithmetic]
          @base-case |
            op(power(g, m), power(g, ZERO)) = power(g, add(m, ZERO))
            [using chain base-lhs-collapses add-m-zero-flipped]

          // reusable recursion facts.
          @power-at-successor |
            forall a: G; forall n: Int; power(a, succ(n)) = op(power(a, n), a)
            [by cite powerSucc]
          @power-at-predecessor |
            forall a: G; forall n: Int; power(a, prev(n)) = op(power(a, n), inv(a))
            [by cite powerPrev]

          // -- successor step: from n=k to n=succ(k) ---------------------------
          @successor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-ih-succ |
                assume op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) {
                  @ih-succ |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-ih-succ]
                  @exponent-succ |
                    add(m, succ(k)) = succ(add(m, k))
                    [using arithmetic]
                  // g^(m + succ k) = g^(succ(m+k)) = op(g^(m+k), g).
                  @rhs-unfolds |
                    power(g, succ(add(m, k))) = op(power(g, add(m, k)), g)
                    [by apply_at(g, add(m, k)) power-at-successor]
                  // g^(succ k) = op(g^k, g).
                  @right-factor |
                    power(g, succ(k)) = op(power(g, k), g)
                    [by apply_at(g, k) power-at-successor]
                  // op(g^m, g^(succ k)) = op(g^m, op(g^k, g)) = op(op(g^m, g^k), g)
                  //   = op(g^(m+k), g) = g^(succ(m+k)) = g^(m + succ k).
                  @reassociates |
                    op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), g)) = op(op(power(g, m), power(g, k)), g)
                    [by symmetry op-assoc-succ]
                  @op-assoc-succ |
                    op(op(power(g, m), power(g, k)), g) = op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), g))
                    [using specialize associative(power(g, m), power(g, k), g)]
                  @lhs-expands |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k))) = op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), g))
                    [by rewrite right-factor op-refl-succ]
                  @op-refl-succ |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k))) = op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k)))
                    [by reflexivity]
                  @conclusion-succ |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k))) = power(g, add(m, succ(k)))
                    [using chain lhs-expands reassociates ih-succ rhs-unfolds exponent-succ]
                }
              @ih-implies-succ |
                op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) -> op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k))) = power(g, add(m, succ(k)))
                [by discharge given-ih-succ]
            }
          @successor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) -> op(power(g, m), power(g, succ(k))) = power(g, add(m, succ(k)))
            [by generalize successor-step]

          // -- predecessor step: from n=k to n=prev(k) ------------------------
          @predecessor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-ih-prev |
                assume op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) {
                  @ih-prev |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-ih-prev]
                  @exponent-prev |
                    add(m, prev(k)) = prev(add(m, k))
                    [using arithmetic]
                  // g^(m + prev k) = g^(prev(m+k)) = op(g^(m+k), g⁻¹).
                  @rhs-unfolds |
                    power(g, prev(add(m, k))) = op(power(g, add(m, k)), inv(g))
                    [by apply_at(g, add(m, k)) power-at-predecessor]
                  // g^(prev k) = op(g^k, g⁻¹).
                  @right-factor |
                    power(g, prev(k)) = op(power(g, k), inv(g))
                    [by apply_at(g, k) power-at-predecessor]
                  @op-assoc-prev |
                    op(op(power(g, m), power(g, k)), inv(g)) = op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), inv(g)))
                    [using specialize associative(power(g, m), power(g, k), inv(g))]
                  @reassociates |
                    op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), inv(g))) = op(op(power(g, m), power(g, k)), inv(g))
                    [by symmetry op-assoc-prev]
                  @lhs-expands |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k))) = op(power(g, m), op(power(g, k), inv(g)))
                    [by rewrite right-factor op-refl-prev]
                  @op-refl-prev |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k))) = op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k)))
                    [by reflexivity]
                  @conclusion-prev |
                    op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k))) = power(g, add(m, prev(k)))
                    [using chain lhs-expands reassociates ih-prev rhs-unfolds exponent-prev]
                }
              @ih-implies-prev |
                op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) -> op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k))) = power(g, add(m, prev(k)))
                [by discharge given-ih-prev]
            }
          @predecessor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, k)) = power(g, add(m, k)) -> op(power(g, m), power(g, prev(k))) = power(g, add(m, prev(k)))
            [by generalize predecessor-step]

          @powers-add-over-all-n |
            forall n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
            [using instantiation induction((fun n: Int => op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n)))) base-case successor-step-all-k predecessor-step-all-k]
        }
      @discharge-m |
        forall m, n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
        [by generalize generalize-m]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g: G; forall m, n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
    [by generalize generalize-g]
qed

theorem exponentLawPower: forall g: G; forall m, n: Int; power(power(g, m), n) = power(g, mul(m, n))
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: G {
      @generalize-m |
        fix m: Int {
          // base n = ZERO: (g^m)^0 = e = g^0 = g^(m·0).
          @gm-power-zero |
            power(power(g, m), ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(power(g, m))]
          @g-power-zero |
            power(g, ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(g)]
          @mul-m-zero |
            mul(m, ZERO) = ZERO
            [using specialize mulZeroRight(m)]
          @g-power-mul-m-zero |
            power(g, mul(m, ZERO)) = e
            [using chain mul-m-zero g-power-zero]
          @e-is-g-power-mul-m-zero |
            e = power(g, mul(m, ZERO))
            [by symmetry g-power-mul-m-zero]
          @base-case |
            power(power(g, m), ZERO) = power(g, mul(m, ZERO))
            [using chain gm-power-zero e-is-g-power-mul-m-zero]

          @gm-at-successor |
            forall n: Int; power(power(g, m), succ(n)) = op(power(power(g, m), n), power(g, m))
            [by apply_at(power(g, m)) power-at-successor-generic]
          @power-at-successor-generic |
            forall a: G; forall n: Int; power(a, succ(n)) = op(power(a, n), a)
            [by cite powerSucc]
          @gm-at-predecessor |
            forall n: Int; power(power(g, m), prev(n)) = op(power(power(g, m), n), inv(power(g, m)))
            [by apply_at(power(g, m)) power-at-predecessor-generic]
          @power-at-predecessor-generic |
            forall a: G; forall n: Int; power(a, prev(n)) = op(power(a, n), inv(a))
            [by cite powerPrev]

          // -- successor step ------------------------------------------------
          @successor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-ih-succ |
                assume power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) {
                  @ih-succ |
                    power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-ih-succ]
                  // (g^m)^(succ k) = op((g^m)^k, g^m) = op(g^(mk), g^m).
                  @lhs-unfolds |
                    power(power(g, m), succ(k)) = op(power(power(g, m), k), power(g, m))
                    [by apply_at(k) gm-at-successor]
                  @fold-ih |
                    op(power(power(g, m), k), power(g, m)) = op(power(g, mul(m, k)), power(g, m))
                    [by rewrite ih-succ gm-succ-refl]
                  @gm-succ-refl |
                    op(power(power(g, m), k), power(g, m)) = op(power(power(g, m), k), power(g, m))
                    [by reflexivity]
                  // op(g^(mk), g^m) = g^(mk + m).
                  @powers-combine |
                    op(power(g, mul(m, k)), power(g, m)) = power(g, add(mul(m, k), m))
                    [using specialize exponentLawProduct(g, mul(m, k), m)]
                  // mul(m, succ k) = add(mul(m,k), m).
                  @mul-succ |
                    mul(m, succ(k)) = add(mul(m, k), m)
                    [using specialize mulSuccRight(m, k)]
                  @exponent-succ |
                    power(g, add(mul(m, k), m)) = power(g, mul(m, succ(k)))
                    [by rewrite mul-succ exponent-succ-refl]
                  @exponent-succ-refl |
                    power(g, add(mul(m, k), m)) = power(g, add(mul(m, k), m))
                    [by reflexivity]
                  @conclusion-succ |
                    power(power(g, m), succ(k)) = power(g, mul(m, succ(k)))
                    [using chain lhs-unfolds fold-ih powers-combine exponent-succ]
                }
              @ih-implies-succ |
                power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) -> power(power(g, m), succ(k)) = power(g, mul(m, succ(k)))
                [by discharge given-ih-succ]
            }
          @successor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) -> power(power(g, m), succ(k)) = power(g, mul(m, succ(k)))
            [by generalize successor-step]

          // -- predecessor step ----------------------------------------------
          @predecessor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-ih-prev |
                assume power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) {
                  @ih-prev |
                    power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-ih-prev]
                  // (g^m)^(prev k) = op((g^m)^k, inv(g^m)) = op(g^(mk), g^(−m)).
                  @lhs-unfolds |
                    power(power(g, m), prev(k)) = op(power(power(g, m), k), inv(power(g, m)))
                    [by apply_at(k) gm-at-predecessor]
                  @neg-inv |
                    power(g, neg(m)) = inv(power(g, m))
                    [using specialize powerNegInverse(g, m)]
                  @inv-as-neg-power |
                    inv(power(g, m)) = power(g, neg(m))
                    [by symmetry neg-inv]
                  @fold-ih |
                    op(power(power(g, m), k), inv(power(g, m))) = op(power(g, mul(m, k)), power(g, neg(m)))
                    [using chain ih-prev inv-as-neg-power]
                  // op(g^(mk), g^(−m)) = g^(mk + (−m)).
                  @powers-combine |
                    op(power(g, mul(m, k)), power(g, neg(m))) = power(g, add(mul(m, k), neg(m)))
                    [using specialize exponentLawProduct(g, mul(m, k), neg(m))]
                  // mul(m, prev k) = add(mul(m,k), neg m).
                  @mul-prev |
                    mul(m, prev(k)) = add(mul(m, k), neg(m))
                    [using specialize mulPrevRight(m, k)]
                  @exponent-prev |
                    power(g, add(mul(m, k), neg(m))) = power(g, mul(m, prev(k)))
                    [by rewrite mul-prev exponent-prev-refl]
                  @exponent-prev-refl |
                    power(g, add(mul(m, k), neg(m))) = power(g, add(mul(m, k), neg(m)))
                    [by reflexivity]
                  @conclusion-prev |
                    power(power(g, m), prev(k)) = power(g, mul(m, prev(k)))
                    [using chain lhs-unfolds fold-ih powers-combine exponent-prev]
                }
              @ih-implies-prev |
                power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) -> power(power(g, m), prev(k)) = power(g, mul(m, prev(k)))
                [by discharge given-ih-prev]
            }
          @predecessor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; power(power(g, m), k) = power(g, mul(m, k)) -> power(power(g, m), prev(k)) = power(g, mul(m, prev(k)))

          @powers-mul-over-all-n |

            [using instantiation induction((fun n: Int => power(power(g, m), n) = power(g, mul(m, n)))) base-case successor-step-all-k predecessor-step-all-k]
        }
      @discharge-m |
        forall m, n: Int; power(power(g, m), n) = power(g, mul(m, n))
        [by generalize generalize-m]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g: G; forall m, n: Int; power(power(g, m), n) = power(g, mul(m, n))
    [by generalize generalize-g]

theorem exponentLawOfProduct: forall g, h: G; forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = power(op(inv(h), inv(g)), neg(n))
proof

    fix g: G {
      @generalize-h |
        fix h: G {
          @generalize-n |
            fix n: Int {
              // (h⁻¹g⁻¹) is the inv of (gh).
              @reversed-product-is-inv |
                inv(op(g, h)) = op(inv(h), inv(g))
                [using specialize invProduct(g, h)]
              // ((gh)⁻¹)^(−n) = (gh)^(−(−n)).

                [using specialize powerInverseIsNegExponent(op(g, h), neg(n))]

                [using arithmetic]
              @conclusion-power-of-product |
                power(op(g, h), n) = power(op(inv(h), inv(g)), neg(n))
                [using chain double-negation-cancels inv-base-pulls-out-sign reversed-product-is-inv]
            }
          @discharge-n |
            forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = power(op(inv(h), inv(g)), neg(n))

        }
      @discharge-h |
        forall h: G; forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = power(op(inv(h), inv(g)), neg(n))
        [by generalize generalize-h]
    }

// (3, abelian)  (gh)^n = g^n h^n  — cites commutativity
theorem exponentLawAbelian: forall g, h: G; forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = op(power(g, n), power(h, n))
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: G {
      @generalize-h |
        fix h: G {
          // base case n = ZERO: (gh)^0 = e = op(e, e) = op(g^0, h^0).
          @product-power-zero |
            power(op(g, h), ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(op(g, h))]
          @g-power-zero |
            power(g, ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(g)]
          @h-power-zero |
            power(h, ZERO) = e
            [using specialize powerZero(h)]
          @identity-times-identity |
            op(e, e) = e
            [using specialize identityLeft(e)]
          @base-case |
            power(op(g, h), ZERO) = op(power(g, ZERO), power(h, ZERO))
            [using simplify product-power-zero identity-times-identity g-power-zero h-power-zero]

          // -- successor step: from n=k to n=succ(k) ---------------------------
          @successor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-inductive-hypothesis |
                assume power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) {
                  @inductive-hypothesis |
                    power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-inductive-hypothesis]
                  // (gh)^(succ k) = op((gh)^k, gh) = op(op(g^k,h^k), op(g,h)).
                  @product-power-unfolds |
                    power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(power(op(g, h), k), op(g, h))
                    [using specialize powerSucc(op(g, h), k)]
                  // fold the IH into the unfolded product: op(op(g^k,h^k), op(g,h)).
                  @product-with-inductive-hypothesis |
                    power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), op(g, h))
                    [by rewrite inductive-hypothesis product-power-unfolds]
                  // regroup op(op(g^k,h^k), op(g,h)) = op(op(g^k,g), op(h^k,h)).
                  @factors-regroup |
                    op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), op(g, h)) = op(op(power(g, k), g), op(power(h, k), h))
                    [using specialize opMiddleSwap(power(g, k), power(h, k), g, h)]
                  @product-regrouped-by-base |
                    power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), g), op(power(h, k), h))
                    [by rewrite factors-regroup product-with-inductive-hypothesis]
                  // g^(succ k) = op(g^k, g); h^(succ k) = op(h^k, h) — refold each base.
                  @g-power-succ |
                    power(g, succ(k)) = op(power(g, k), g)
                    [using specialize powerSucc(g, k)]
                  @h-power-succ |
                    power(h, succ(k)) = op(power(h, k), h)
                    [using specialize powerSucc(h, k)]
                  @g-factor-refolded |
                    power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(power(g, succ(k)), op(power(h, k), h))
                    [by rewrite g-power-succ product-regrouped-by-base]
                  @conclusion-successor |
                    power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(power(g, succ(k)), power(h, succ(k)))
                    [by rewrite h-power-succ g-factor-refolded]
                }
              @inductive-hypothesis-implies-successor |
                power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) -> power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(power(g, succ(k)), power(h, succ(k)))
                [by discharge given-inductive-hypothesis]
            }
          @successor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) -> power(op(g, h), succ(k)) = op(power(g, succ(k)), power(h, succ(k)))
            [by generalize successor-step]

          // -- predecessor step: from n=k to n=prev(k) ------------------------
          @predecessor-step |
            fix k: Int {
              @given-inductive-hypothesis |
                assume power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) {
                  @inductive-hypothesis |
                    power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k))
                    [by hypothesis given-inductive-hypothesis]
                  // (gh)^(prev k) = op((gh)^k, (gh)⁻¹) = op(op(g^k,h^k), (gh)⁻¹).
                  @product-power-unfolds |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(power(op(g, h), k), inv(op(g, h)))
                    [using specialize powerPrev(op(g, h), k)]
                  // fold the IH into the unfolded product: op(op(g^k,h^k), (gh)⁻¹).
                  @product-with-inductive-hypothesis |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), inv(op(g, h)))
                    [by rewrite inductive-hypothesis product-power-unfolds]
                  // (gh)⁻¹ = h⁻¹g⁻¹.
                  @inv-of-product |
                    inv(op(g, h)) = op(inv(h), inv(g))
                    [using specialize invProduct(g, h)]
                  @product-with-reversed-inverses |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), op(inv(h), inv(g)))
                    [by rewrite inv-of-product product-with-inductive-hypothesis]
                  // h⁻¹g⁻¹ = g⁻¹h⁻¹ (commutative).
                  @inverses-commute |
                    op(inv(h), inv(g)) = op(inv(g), inv(h))
                    [using specialize commutative(inv(h), inv(g))]
                  @product-with-ordered-inverses |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), op(inv(g), inv(h)))
                    [by rewrite inverses-commute product-with-reversed-inverses]
                  // regroup op(op(g^k,h^k), op(g⁻¹,h⁻¹)) = op(op(g^k,g⁻¹), op(h^k,h⁻¹)).
                  @factors-regroup |
                    op(op(power(g, k), power(h, k)), op(inv(g), inv(h))) = op(op(power(g, k), inv(g)), op(power(h, k), inv(h)))
                    [using specialize opMiddleSwap(power(g, k), power(h, k), inv(g), inv(h))]
                  @product-regrouped-by-base |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(op(power(g, k), inv(g)), op(power(h, k), inv(h)))
                    [by rewrite factors-regroup product-with-ordered-inverses]
                  // g^(prev k) = op(g^k, g⁻¹); h^(prev k) = op(h^k, h⁻¹) — refold each base.
                  @g-power-prev |
                    power(g, prev(k)) = op(power(g, k), inv(g))
                    [using specialize powerPrev(g, k)]
                  @h-power-prev |
                    power(h, prev(k)) = op(power(h, k), inv(h))
                    [using specialize powerPrev(h, k)]
                  @conclusion-predecessor-g |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(power(g, prev(k)), op(power(h, k), inv(h)))
                    [by rewrite g-power-prev product-regrouped-by-base]
                  @conclusion-predecessor |
                    power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(power(g, prev(k)), power(h, prev(k)))
                    [by rewrite h-power-prev conclusion-predecessor-g]
                }
              @inductive-hypothesis-implies-predecessor |
                power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) -> power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(power(g, prev(k)), power(h, prev(k)))
                [by discharge given-inductive-hypothesis]
            }
          @predecessor-step-all-k |
            forall k: Int; power(op(g, h), k) = op(power(g, k), power(h, k)) -> power(op(g, h), prev(k)) = op(power(g, prev(k)), power(h, prev(k)))
            [by generalize predecessor-step]

          @product-power-distributes-over-all-n |
            forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = op(power(g, n), power(h, n))
            [using instantiation induction((fun n: Int => power(op(g, h), n) = op(power(g, n), power(h, n)))) base-case successor-step-all-k predecessor-step-all-k]
        }
      @discharge-h |
        forall h: G; forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = op(power(g, n), power(h, n))
        [by generalize generalize-h]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g, h: G; forall n: Int; power(op(g, h), n) = op(power(g, n), power(h, n))
    [by generalize generalize-g]
qed
```

Notice that $(gh)^n \neq g^nh^n$ in general, since the group may not be abelian. If the group is ${\mathbb Z}$ or ${\mathbb Z}_n$, we write the group operation additively and the exponential operation multiplicatively; that is, we write $ng$ instead of $g^n$. The laws of exponents now become

1. $mg + ng = (m+n)g$ for all $m, n \in {\mathbb Z}$;
2. $m(ng) = (mn)g$ for all $m, n \in {\mathbb Z}$;
3. $m(g + h) = mg + mh$ for all $n \in {\mathbb Z}$.

It is important to realize that the last statement can be made only because ${\mathbb Z}$ and ${\mathbb Z}_n$ are commutative groups.

### Historical Note

Although the first clear axiomatic definition of a group was not given until the late 1800s, group-theoretic methods had been employed before this time in the development of many areas of mathematics, including geometry and the theory of algebraic equations.

Joseph-Louis Lagrange used group-theoretic methods in a 1770–1771 memoir to study methods of solving polynomial equations. Later, Évariste Galois (1811–1832) succeeded in developing the mathematics necessary to determine exactly which polynomial equations could be solved in terms of the coefficients of the polynomial. Galois' primary tool was group theory.

The study of geometry was revolutionized in 1872 when Felix Klein proposed that geometric spaces should be studied by examining those properties that are invariant under a transformation of the space. Sophus Lie, a contemporary of Klein, used group theory to study solutions of partial differential equations. One of the first modern treatments of group theory appeared in William Burnside's *The Theory of Groups of Finite Order* [1], first published in 1897.
