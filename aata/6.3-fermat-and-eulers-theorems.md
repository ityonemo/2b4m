## The Euler φ-function

**Definition.** The **Euler φ-function** is the map
$\varphi : \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$ defined by $\varphi(n) = 1$ for
$n = 1$, and, for $n > 1$, $\varphi(n)$ is the number of positive integers $m$ with
$1 \leq m < n$ and $\gcd(m,n) = 1$.

**Theorem 6.16.** Let $U(n)$ be the group of units in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. Then
$|U(n)| = \varphi(n)$.

As in §6.2, a count here is a **listing** rather than a cardinal, so "$|U(n)| =
\varphi(n)$" becomes: splitting $\mathbb{Z}_n$'s listing at `isUnit` lists the units
without repetition, and *that* length is what $\varphi(n)$ names. The split is
`std/integer/mod-n-listing.b4m`'s `znSplitsAtUnits`.

```2b4m
import mod_n_listing <<< "std/integer/mod-n-listing.b4m"

// U(n) listed: ℤ_n's listing split at `isUnit` — the units on one side, the
// non-units on the other, with the two lengths adding to n. The units' length IS φ(n).
theorem unitsAreListed = mod_n_listing.znSplitsAtUnits
```

For example, $|U(12)| = \varphi(12) = 4$ since the numbers that are relatively prime
to 12 are 1, 5, 7, and 11. For any prime $p$, $\varphi(p) = p-1$.

## Theorem 6.17 — Euler's Theorem

**Theorem 6.17 (Euler's Theorem).** Let $a$ and $n$ be integers such that $n > 0$
and $\gcd(a, n) = 1$. Then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.

*Proof (Judson).* By Theorem 6.16 the order of $U(n)$ is $\varphi(n)$.
Consequently, $a^{\varphi(n)} = 1$ for all $a \in U(n)$; or $a^{\varphi(n)} - 1$ is
divisible by $n$. Therefore, $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$. $\blacksquare$

Judson's proof is a group-theoretic fact applied in $U(n)$: **an element raised to
the size of its group is the identity.** That fact is where the mathematics lives, so
it is proved once, over an arbitrary finite group, in
`std/group/order-divides.b4m` — and Euler's congruence is what it *says* when the
group is $U(n)$, where the size is $\varphi(n)$ and the identity is 1.

```2b4m
import order_divides <<< "std/group/order-divides.b4m"

// a^|G| = e in any finite group. The order of a divides |G| (Corollary 6.6), and a
// multiple of the order is an exponent returning the identity — which is Euler.
theorem anElementToTheGroupsLengthIsTheIdentity = order_divides.anElementToTheGroupsLengthIsTheIdentity
```

The step Judson compresses into "consequently" is the engine, isolated:

```2b4m
// `hasOrder(g, d)` already carries `power(g, d) = e`, so from d | n — say n = d·k —
// `powerMul` gives power(g, n) = power(power(g, d), k) = power(e, k) = e.
theorem aMultipleOfTheOrderIsAnIdentityExponent = order_divides.aMultipleOfTheOrderIsAnIdentityExponent
```

And the divisibility it rests on is Corollary 6.6 from §6.2:

```2b4m
theorem theOrderDividesTheGroupsLength = order_divides.theOrderDividesTheGroupsLength
```

**What is transliterated and what is not.** The group-theoretic content of Euler's
Theorem — the whole of Judson's proof — is machine-checked above. Rendering it as
the *congruence* $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ additionally needs: (i) a
`phi` function defined as the length of the units' listing, which requires knowing
that length is unique (§6.2's `anyTwoGroupListingsHaveTheSameLength`, transported to
$U(n)$); and (ii) the bridge from $\mathbb{Z}_n$'s equality to congruence of integer
representatives. Neither is new mathematics, and neither is built yet.

## Theorem 6.18 — Fermat's Little Theorem

**Theorem 6.18 (Fermat's Little Theorem).** Let $p$ be any prime number and suppose
that $p \nmid a$. Then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. Furthermore, for any integer
$b$, $b^p \equiv b \pmod{p}$.

Fermat is the special case of Euler's Theorem with $n = p$ prime, using
$\varphi(p) = p - 1$. The group fact is therefore already proved above; what Fermat
needs beyond Euler is the *count* — that exactly $p-1$ of the classes
$1, \ldots, p-1$ are units mod a prime. That counting argument is not built, so
Fermat is recorded here as prose and its proof deferred.

Note that $b^p \equiv b \pmod p$ is the extension to *every* $b$: where $p \nmid b$
it follows from $b^{p-1} \equiv 1$ by multiplying through by $b$, and where $p \mid b$
both sides are $0$.
