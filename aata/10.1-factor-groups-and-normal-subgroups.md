The theory lives in `std/group/normal.b4m`, layered on `std/subgroup.b4m` and
`std/group/coset.b4m`.

## Normal subgroups

**Definition.** A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is **normal** in $G$ if $gH = Hg$ for all
$g \in G$. That is, a normal subgroup of a group $G$ is one in which the right and left
cosets are precisely the same.

A coset is not a *set* here — `2b4m` has no sort of subsets of a group — so $gH$ is
represented by its **membership predicate**, as in §6.1. "$gH = Hg$" is therefore
extensional agreement of the left- and right-coset predicates, which is exactly how
Judson uses it: he never needs a coset as an object, only membership and equality.

```2b4m
import normal <<< "std/group/normal.b4m"

sort G = normal.Grp
const e = normal.E
func op = normal.op
func inv = normal.inverse

// H, as the membership predicate characterizing it
pred inH = normal.inSubgroup

// x ∈ gH and x ∈ Hg
define inLeftCoset(g, x) = normal.inCoset(g, x)
define inRightCoset(g, x) = normal.inRightCoset(g, x)
```

**Judson's examples.** Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal, since $gh = hg$
makes $gH = Hg$ automatic. In $S_3$, the subgroup $\{(1), (12)\}$ is *not* normal —
$(123)H = \{(123),(13)\}$ while $H(123) = \{(123),(23)\}$ — but
$N = \{(1),(123),(132)\}$ is, since its two cosets are $N$ itself and
$(12)N = N(12) = \{(12),(13),(23)\}$. These are computations in a specific group; the
general characterization is below.

## Theorem 10.3 — the conjugation characterization

**Theorem 10.3.** Let $G$ be a group and $N$ be a subgroup of $G$. Then the following
statements are equivalent.

1. The subgroup $N$ is normal in $G$.
2. For all $g \in G$, $gNg^{-1} \subset N$.
3. For all $g \in G$, $gNg^{-1} = N$.

*Proof (Judson).* (1) ⇒ (2). Since $N$ is normal in $G$, $gN = Ng$ for all $g \in G$.
Hence, for a given $g \in G$ and $n \in N$, there exists an $n'$ in $N$ such that
$gn = n'g$. Therefore, $gng^{-1} = n' \in N$, or $gNg^{-1} \subset N$.

(2) ⇒ (3). Let $g \in G$. Since $gNg^{-1} \subset N$, we need only show
$N \subset gNg^{-1}$. For $n \in N$, $g^{-1}n(g^{-1})^{-1} \in N$. Hence,
$g^{-1}ng = n'$ for some $n' \in N$, and therefore $n = gn'g^{-1}$ is in $gNg^{-1}$.
$\blacksquare$

With cosets as predicates, (2) and (3) coincide — containment of predicates in both
directions *is* agreement — so the theorem is the `iff` between the coset form and the
conjugation form. Both directions follow Judson's text: forward via "there exists $n'$
with $gn = n'g$", backward by conjugating with $g^{-1}$ and collapsing
$(g^{-1})^{-1} = g$.

```2b4m
fact conjugationLandsInTheSubgroupIffCosetsCoincide =
  normal.conjugationLandsInTheSubgroupIffCosetsCoincide
```

## Factor groups

**Definition.** If $N$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, the cosets of $N$ in $G$ form a
group $G/N$ — the **factor group** of $G$ mod $N$ — under
$(aN)(bN) = abN$, of order $[G:N]$.

**Theorem 10.4.** Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of a group $G$. The cosets of $N$ in $G$
form a group $G/N$ of order $[G:N]$.

*Proof (Judson).* The group operation on $G/N$ is $(aN)(bN) = abN$. This operation must
be shown to be well-defined; that is, group multiplication must be independent of the
choice of coset representative. Let $aN = bN$ and $cN = dN$. We must show that
$$(aN)(cN) = acN = bdN = (bN)(dN).$$
Then $a = bn_1$ and $c = dn_2$ for some $n_1, n_2 \in N$. Hence
$$acN = bn_1dn_2N = bn_1dN = bn_1Nd = bNd = bdN.$$
$\blacksquare$

Well-definedness is the content, and it is what the library proves. Since
"$aN = bN$" is `inH(inv(a) · b)` (§6.1's
`cosetsCoincideIffQuotientIsInTheSubgroup`), the statement reads: if $a, b$ represent
the same coset and $c, d$ do, then $ac$ and $bd$ do. Judson's middle step
$bn_1N = bNn_1 = bN$ is where normality enters, and it appears as the hypothesis.

```2b4m
fact theFactorOperationIsWellDefined = normal.theFactorOperationIsWellDefined
```

**What is not transliterated.** That $G/N$ is a *group* — associativity, identity $N$,
inverse $a^{-1}N$ — and that its order is $[G:N]$. The first needs the cosets
themselves as a sort to carry the operation (there is none, which is why the operation
appears above as a statement about representatives); the second is Lagrange, already
proved in §6.2 as `theSubgroupsLengthDividesTheGroups`. See `CHAPTER10-PAINPOINTS`.
