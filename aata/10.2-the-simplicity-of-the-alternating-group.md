## Simple groups

**Definition.** Of special interest are groups with no nontrivial normal subgroups.
Such groups are called **simple groups**.

> *There is no sort of subgroups here to quantify over, so simplicity is stated per
> subgroup — as the disjunction below, for a given membership predicate — rather than as
> "for all normal $N$".*

```2b4m
import normal <<< "std/group/normal.b4m"

sort G = normal.Grp
const e = normal.E
func op = normal.op
func inv = normal.inverse
pred inN = normal.inSubgroup

// N is trivial, or N is everything — the two normal subgroups every group has, and
// (for a simple group) the only ones.
define isTrivialSubgroup = normal.isTrivialSubgroup
define isTheWholeGroup = normal.isTheWholeGroup
define isSimpleWitness = normal.isSimpleWitness
```

Of course, we already have a whole class of examples of simple groups: $\mathbb{Z}_p$,
where $p$ is prime. These groups are trivially simple since they have no proper
subgroups other than the subgroup consisting solely of the identity.

The two subgroups the definition names — the trivial one and the whole group — are both
normal.

## Towards $A_n$

Other examples of simple groups are not so easily found. We can, however, show that the
alternating group $A_n$ is simple for $n \geq 5$. The proof requires several lemmas.

**Lemma 10.8.** The alternating group $A_n$ is generated by 3-cycles for $n \geq 3$.

**Proof.** To show that the 3-cycles generate $A_n$, we need only show that any pair of
transpositions can be written as the product of 3-cycles. Since $(a,b) = (b,a)$, every
pair of transpositions must be one of
$$(a,b)(a,b) = \text{id}, \quad (a,b)(c,d) = (a,c,b)(a,c,d), \quad (a,b)(a,c) = (a,c,b).$$
$\blacksquare$

"Every pair must be one of" these three is the trichotomy on how $\{a,b\}$ meets
$\{c,d\}$: equal as sets, sharing one point, or disjoint. Enumerating every assignment of
four points gives seven equality patterns — one disjoint, four sharing a point, two
equal — so the case split is exhaustive.

The step from "every pair is a product of 3-cycles" to the lemma is an induction on the
number of pairs, which is what the even length supplies: an even permutation's
factorization has length $2j$, and each step peels the last two entries.

> NB: This theorem exists in std in `std/permutation/alternating.b4m:3347:everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles`.

```2b4m
import alternating <<< "std/permutation/alternating.b4m"

sort Fn = alternating.Fn
pred inAlternating = alternating.inAlternating
define isAThreeCycle(g) = alternating.isAThreeCycle(g)
define isAProductOfThreeCycles(g) = alternating.isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)

theorem everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles: forall g: Fn;
  inAlternating(g) -> isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: Fn {
      @given-in-the-alternating-group |
        assume inAlternating(g) {
          @in-the-alternating-group |
            inAlternating(g)
            [by hypothesis given-in-the-alternating-group]
          @membership-unfolds |
            inAlternating(g) iff (invertible(g) and isEvenPerm(g))
            [using specialize inAlternatingDef(g)]
          @g-is-even |
            isEvenPerm(g)
            [using tautology membership-unfolds in-the-alternating-group]
          @evenness-unfolds |
            isEvenPerm(g) iff (exists s: Seq; exists m: Nat;
              allTranspositions(s, m) and productUpTo(s, m) = g and divides(TWO, m))
            [using specialize isEvenPermDef(g)]
          @a-factorization-exists |
            exists s: Seq; exists m: Nat;
              allTranspositions(s, m) and productUpTo(s, m) = g and divides(TWO, m)
            [using tautology evenness-unfolds g-is-even]
          @with-the-factorization |
            unpack s: Seq from a-factorization-exists {
              @a-length-exists |
                exists m: Nat;
                  allTranspositions(s, m) and productUpTo(s, m) = g and divides(TWO, m)
                [by hypothesis with-the-factorization]
              @with-the-length |
                unpack m: Nat from a-length-exists {
                  @the-factorization-facts |
                    allTranspositions(s, m) and productUpTo(s, m) = g and divides(TWO, m)
                    [by hypothesis with-the-length]
                  @the-entries-and-the-product |
                    allTranspositions(s, m) and productUpTo(s, m) = g
                    [by and_lhs the-factorization-facts]
                  @the-length-is-even |
                    divides(TWO, m)
                    [by and_rhs the-factorization-facts]
                  @the-entries-are-transpositions |
                    allTranspositions(s, m)
                    [by and_lhs the-entries-and-the-product]
                  @the-product-is-g |
                    productUpTo(s, m) = g
                    [by and_rhs the-entries-and-the-product]
                  @the-entries-unfold |
                    allTranspositions(s, m) iff
                      (forall i: Nat; less_than(i, m) -> isTransposition(at(s, i)))
                    [using specialize allTranspositionsDef(s, m)]
                  @every-entry-is-a-transposition |
                    forall i: Nat; less_than(i, m) -> isTransposition(at(s, i))
                    [using tautology the-entries-unfold the-entries-are-transpositions]
                  // `divides` is transparent: divides(TWO, m) IS exists k; m = mul(TWO, k).
                  @with-the-half-length |
                    unpack j: Nat from the-length-is-even {
                      @the-length-is-a-double |
                        m = mul(TWO, j)
                        [by hypothesis with-the-half-length]
                      @every-entry-below-the-double-is-a-transposition |
                        forall i: Nat; less_than(i, mul(TWO, j)) -> isTransposition(at(s, i))
                        [by rewrite the-length-is-a-double every-entry-is-a-transposition]
                      @the-double-product-is-a-run |
                        isAProductOfThreeCycles(productUpTo(s, mul(TWO, j)))
                        [using specialize anEvenRunOfTranspositionsIsAProductOfThreeCycles(s, j) every-entry-below-the-double-is-a-transposition]
                      @the-product-is-a-run |
                        isAProductOfThreeCycles(productUpTo(s, m))
                        [by rewrite the-length-is-a-double the-double-product-is-a-run]
                      @conclusion-at-the-half-length |
                        isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
                        [by rewrite the-product-is-g the-product-is-a-run]
                    }
                  @conclusion-at-the-length |
                    isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
                    [by unpacked with-the-half-length]
                }
              @conclusion-at-the-factorization |
                isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
                [by unpacked with-the-length]
            }
          @conclusion-is-a-run |
            isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
            [by unpacked with-the-factorization]
        }
      @conclusion-for-g |
        inAlternating(g) -> isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
        [by discharge given-in-the-alternating-group]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g: Fn; inAlternating(g) -> isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)
    [by generalize generalize-g]
qed
```

**Lemma 10.9.** Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of $A_n$, where $n \geq 3$. If $N$
contains a 3-cycle, then $N = A_n$.

**Proof.** We will first show that $A_n$ is generated by 3-cycles of the specific form
$(i,j,k)$, where $i$ and $j$ are fixed in $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ and $k$ varies. Every
3-cycle is the product of 3-cycles of this form, since
$$(i,a,j) = (i,j,a)^2, \quad (i,a,b) = (i,j,b)(i,j,a)^2,$$
$$(j,a,b) = (i,j,b)^2(i,j,a), \quad (a,b,c) = (i,j,a)^2(i,j,c)(i,j,b)^2(i,j,a).$$
Now suppose $N$ is a nontrivial normal subgroup of $A_n$ for $n \geq 3$ containing a
3-cycle of the form $(i,j,a)$. Using the normality of $N$,
$$[(i,j)(a,k)](i,j,a)^2[(i,j)(a,k)]^{-1} = (i,j,k)$$
is in $N$. Hence $N$ contains all of the 3-cycles $(i,j,k)$ for $1 \leq k \leq n$. By
Lemma 10.8 these generate $A_n$; hence $N = A_n$. $\blacksquare$

The half that follows from Lemma 10.8 is that a subgroup of $A_n$ containing every
3-cycle is all of $A_n$: every even permutation is a run of 3-cycles, and a subgroup
contains the product of a run it contains. What remains is the use of normality — from
one 3-cycle in $N$ to every 3-cycle, by conjugation, which turns on
$\sigma(a,b,c)\sigma^{-1} = (\sigma a, \sigma b, \sigma c)$ and on the existence, for
any two triples of distinct points, of a $\sigma \in A_n$ carrying one to the other. The
latter is where $n \geq 5$ enters: a $\sigma$ in $S_n$ always exists, and fixing its
parity needs two spare points to swap.

**Lemma 10.10.** For $n \geq 5$, every nontrivial normal subgroup $N$ of $A_n$ contains
a 3-cycle.

**Proof.** Let $\sigma$ be an arbitrary element of $N$. There are several possible cycle
structures for $\sigma$: it is a 3-cycle; or $\sigma = \tau(a_1, \ldots, a_r)$ with
$r > 3$; or $\sigma = \tau(a_1a_2a_3)(a_4a_5a_6)$; or $\sigma = \tau(a_1a_2a_3)$ with
$\tau$ a product of disjoint 2-cycles; or $\sigma = \tau(a_1a_2)(a_3a_4)$ with $\tau$ a
product of an even number of disjoint 2-cycles. Each case produces a 3-cycle: the second
via $\sigma^{-1}(a_1a_2a_3)\sigma(a_1a_2a_3)^{-1} = (a_1a_3a_r)$, the third by building a
5-cycle and reducing to the second, the fourth by $\sigma^2$, the fifth by two
conjugations. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 10.11.** The alternating group $A_n$ is simple for $n \geq 5$.

**Proof.** Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of $A_n$. By Lemma 10.10, $N$ contains a
3-cycle; by Lemma 10.9, $N = A_n$. Therefore $A_n$ contains no proper nontrivial normal
subgroups for $n \geq 5$. $\blacksquare$

Two hypotheses of the argument are worth naming. The first is $n \geq 5$, and Lemma
10.9's conjugations are the only place the chapter spends it: they need a point to move
to and a point to park the displaced one on, so for any four points a fifth must exist.

The second is that $\sigma$'s cycle structure is one of the five listed. Judson's text
says "There are several possible cycle structures for $\sigma$" and gives the list
without argument; exhaustiveness is asserted rather than proved, so the disjunction of
the five shapes enters here as a hypothesis.

## Historical Note

One of the foremost problems of group theory has been to classify all simple finite groups.
This problem is over a century old and has been solved only in the last few decades of the
twentieth century. In a sense, finite simple groups are the building blocks of all finite
groups. The first nonabelian simple groups to be discovered were the alternating groups.
Galois was the first to prove that $A_5$ was simple. Later, mathematicians such as C. Jordan
and L. E. Dickson found several infinite families of matrix groups that were simple. Other
families of simple groups were discovered in the 1950s. At the turn of the century, William
Burnside conjectured that all nonabelian simple groups must have even order. In 1963, W. Feit
and J. Thompson proved Burnside's conjecture and published their results in the paper
*Solvability of Groups of Odd Order*, which appeared in the *Pacific Journal of Mathematics*.
Their proof, running over 250 pages, gave impetus to a program in the 1960s and 1970s to
classify all finite simple groups. Daniel Gorenstein was the organizer of this remarkable
effort. One of the last simple groups was the Monster, discovered by R. Greiss. The Monster,
a $196{,}833 \times 196{,}833$ matrix group, is one of the 26 sporadic, or special, simple
groups. These sporadic simple groups are groups that fit into no infinite family of simple
groups. Some of the sporadic groups play an important role in physics.
