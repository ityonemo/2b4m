## Simple groups

**Definition.** Of special interest are groups with no nontrivial normal subgroups.
Such groups are called **simple groups**.

Simplicity is stated here *per subgroup* rather than as "for all normal $N$": `2b4m`
has no sort of subgroups to quantify over, the same limit §9.1 met for $\cong$. So the
property is the disjunction below, proved by a consumer for its own membership
predicate — which is how `std/subgroup.b4m` handles subgroups throughout.

```2b4m
import normal <<< "std/group/normal.b4m"

sort G = normal.Grp
const e = normal.E
func op = normal.op
func inv = normal.inverse
pred inN = normal.inSubgroup

// N is trivial, or N is everything — the two normal subgroups every group has, and
// (for a simple group) the only ones.
define isTrivialSubgroup = normal.isTrivialSubgroup
define isTheWholeGroup = normal.isTheWholeGroup
define isSimpleWitness = normal.isSimpleWitness
```

Of course, we already have a whole class of examples: $\mathbb{Z}_p$ for $p$ prime.
These are trivially simple since they have no proper subgroups other than the identity
— which §6.2's `primeOrderGroupIsGeneratedByAnyNonIdentity` is the substance of.

The definition is not vacuous: the two subgroups it names are both genuinely normal.

```2b4m
fact theTrivialSubgroupIsNormal = normal.theTrivialSubgroupIsNormal
fact aNormalSubgroupContainingEveryGeneratorIsTheWholeGroup =
  normal.aNormalSubgroupContainingEveryGeneratorIsTheWholeGroup
```

## Towards $A_n$

Other examples of simple groups are not so easily found. We can, however, show that the
alternating group $A_n$ is simple for $n \geq 5$. The proof requires several lemmas.

**Lemma 10.8.** The alternating group $A_n$ is generated by 3-cycles for $n \geq 3$.

*Proof (Judson).* We need only show that any pair of transpositions can be written as
the product of 3-cycles. Since $(a,b) = (b,a)$, every pair of transpositions must be
one of
$$(a,b)(a,b) = \text{id}, \quad (a,b)(c,d) = (a,c,b)(a,c,d), \quad (a,b)(a,c) = (a,c,b).$$
$\blacksquare$

This lemma is machine-checked, as `everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles` in
`std/permutation/alternating.b4m`. Two notes on the transliteration.

Judson's "every pair must be one of" these three is the trichotomy on how $\{a,b\}$ meets
$\{c,d\}$ — equal as sets, sharing one point, or disjoint. Enumerating every assignment of
four points gives seven reachable equality patterns (one disjoint, four sharing a point,
two equal), so the case split is *proved* exhaustive rather than asserted.

And Judson's informal step from "every pair is a product of 3-cycles" to the lemma is an
induction on the number of PAIRS, which is exactly what the even length supplies: an even
permutation's factorization has length $2j$, and each step peels the last two entries.
That induction, not the three identities, is the bulk of the work.

```2b4m
import alternating <<< "std/permutation/alternating.b4m"

sort Fn = alternating.Fn
pred inAlternating = alternating.inAlternating
define isAThreeCycle(g) = alternating.isAThreeCycle(g)
define isAProductOfThreeCycles(g) = alternating.isAProductOfThreeCycles(g)

fact everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles =
  alternating.everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles
```

**Lemma 10.9.** Let $N$ be a normal subgroup of $A_n$, where $n \geq 3$. If $N$
contains a 3-cycle, then $N = A_n$.

The half of this that follows from Lemma 10.8 is machine-checked: a subgroup of $A_n$
containing EVERY 3-cycle is all of $A_n$ (`aSubgroupContainingEveryThreeCycleContainsThe`
`AlternatingGroup`), since every even permutation is a run of 3-cycles and a subgroup
contains the product of a run it contains. It is stated over a schema parameter `inN(Fn)`
rather than through a `model`, because $N$ here is a hypothesis rather than a fixed
predicate — so a consumer supplies its own membership predicate.

What that leaves is Judson's use of NORMALITY: from one 3-cycle in $N$ to every 3-cycle, by
conjugation. `conjugateOfACycle` already gives $\sigma(a,b,c)\sigma^{-1} =
(\sigma a, \sigma b, \sigma c)$, so what remains is that for any two triples of distinct
points some $\sigma \in A_n$ carries one to the other. That is where the $n \geq 5$
hypothesis enters — a $\sigma$ in $S_n$ always exists, and fixing its parity needs two
spare points to swap — and it is the same finite-support machinery Lemma 10.10 needs, so
it is shared rather than extra work.

**Lemma 10.10.** For $n \geq 5$, every nontrivial normal subgroup $N$ of $A_n$ contains
a 3-cycle.

**Theorem 10.11.** The alternating group $A_n$ is simple for $n \geq 5$.

The composition is machine-checked in `std/permutation/simplicity.b4m`:
`aNormalSubgroupWithAFamilyMemberIsTheAlternatingGroup` takes a normal subgroup $N$ containing
one member of the fixed family $(i,j,k)$ and concludes $N = A_n$. It chains Lemma 10.9 (one
family member gives *every* 3-cycle) into Lemma 10.8 (every even permutation is a product of
3-cycles, so a subgroup with all of them has all of $A_n$).

What is **not** yet discharged is the premise Lemma 10.10 owes — that a nontrivial normal
subgroup contains a 3-cycle at all. Its algebra is proved; the bookkeeping is not. See "What is
proved, and what is not" below.

## What is proved, and what is not

The **general** half of Judson's argument is machine-checked above: what simplicity says,
and that both named subgroups are normal. Note that
`aNormalSubgroupContainingEveryGeneratorIsTheWholeGroup` is NOT Lemma 10.9 despite the
name — it is a tautological unfold of `isTheWholeGroup`, taking "every element is in $N$"
to "$N$ is everything". The real content of Lemma 10.9 is getting that premise, which is
what Lemma 10.8 plus normality supply.

The **$A_n$-specific** half is in progress. Both reasons originally recorded here for why it
could not start have since been retired, and the record of each is worth keeping because both
were wrong in the same way — a missing-machinery claim that a probe disproved.

1. **"$A_n$ has no home in `std`."** It does now: `std/permutation/alternating.b4m` defines
   `isEvenPerm` / `isOddPerm` / `inAlternating` as real predicates and proves $A_n$ closed
   under composition, inverses and the identity, with parity well-defined. So §10.2's
   statements can name their subject. This was only ever file placement, as recorded.

2. **"Lemma 10.10 needs cycle arithmetic over variable support."** It does not. The "variable
   support" framing was the error: extensionality reduces each identity to a case analysis on
   EQUALITY OF POINTS, and `std/permutation/parity.b4m` already carries the pointwise deciders.
   All four of Lemma 10.8's identities are proved in
   `std/permutation/alternating-identities.b4m` (41 theorems), because std's cycle fold makes a
   3-cycle a composite of transpositions — `cycleSucc` gives
   $(a_0a_1a_2) = (a_0a_2) \circ (a_0a_1)$ — so no new sort is needed.

   The $r > 3$ case looked like it needed induction on $r$. It does not: conjugating a 3-cycle
   relabels its THREE points, so `conjugateOfACycle` (already proved for arbitrary length)
   applies, and $r$ enters only through $\sigma^{-1}(a_1) = a_r$. The inverse's action on a
   cycle's own points is `aCycleInverseRetreats` / `aCycleInverseWrapsBackward`
   (`std/permutation/cycle.b4m`).

**Lemma 10.8 is now proved** (`everyEvenPermutationIsAProductOfThreeCycles`), and with it
the half of Lemma 10.9 that does not use normality — see those sections above. What remains
is two things, and they share their machinery:

- **Lemma 10.9's conjugation step** is now *half* proved. The part that is pure algebra —
  that a conjugate of a 3-cycle IS a 3-cycle — is
  `aConjugateOfAThreeCycleIsAThreeCycle` (`std/permutation/alternating.b4m`): in std's fold a
  3-cycle is $T(x,z) \circ T(x,y)$, so conjugating one is conjugating a product of two
  transpositions, which `conjugationIsMultiplicative` splits and
  `conjugateOfATransposition` moves onto the images, with injectivity carrying the three
  points' distinctness across. Specialized to $A_n$ — where normality is
  `theAlternatingGroupIsClosedUnderConjugation`, itself just closure under composition and
  inverses — this gives
  `aConjugateOfAThreeCycleIsAThreeCycleInTheAlternatingGroup` unconditionally.

  **Transitivity** — that conjugation reaches *every* 3-cycle from a given one — is now proved
  too, and it needed no parity repair. The obstacle was the framing: "any triple of distinct
  points maps to any other by some even $\sigma$" suggests building $\sigma$ from a prescribed
  finite map. But conjugation acts on 3-*cycles*, not triples, and a 3-cycle equals each of its
  rotations, so a target is matched only up to rotation. With that, there is a uniform
  conjugator, and it is itself a 3-cycle:
  $$(a,b,c)\,(i,j,a)\,(a,b,c)^{-1} = (i,j,b).$$
  Being a 3-cycle it is a product of exactly two transpositions, hence even by construction and
  already in $A_n$ — so $\sigma$'s parity never needs fixing. Here $b$ and $c$ are the two
  points outside $\{i,j,a\}$, which is the honest place $n \geq 5$ is spent: a point to move
  *to*, and a point to park the displaced one on. This is
  `conjugatingAThreeCycleMovesItsThirdPoint`.

  The orbit has one far point, and it needs its own conjugator. Breadth-first search over all
  twenty 3-cycles on five points under that move gives diameter 3, and the distance-3 target is
  always the starting 3-cycle's **twist** $(i,a,j)$ — every other 3-cycle is two moves or
  fewer. So the twist gets a direct conjugator rather than a chain:
  $$\bigl(T(i,j)\,T(b,c)\bigr)\,(i,j,a)\,\bigl(T(i,j)\,T(b,c)\bigr)^{-1} = (i,a,j).$$
  Here the conjugator does two unrelated jobs, and separating them is the point. $T(i,j)$ does
  the mathematics: it swaps two of the 3-cycle's points and fixes the third, and the
  relabelling under that *is* the twist. $T(b,c)$ touches the 3-cycle not at all — $b$ and $c$
  lie outside $\{i,j,a\}$ — and exists only to make the conjugator **even**, so that it lies
  in $A_n$; a single transposition would relabel identically and be odd. This is
  `conjugatingAThreeCycleByASwapAndASpareSwapGivesItsTwist`, and it is the one place the two
  spare points are spent on parity.
- **Lemma 10.10's algebra is now proved, and it needs no classification theorem.** Judson
presents five cases by CYCLE TYPE, and that framing is what made this look like it required
classifying $\sigma$ by the multiset of its cycle lengths. It does not. Every case's proof
reads $\sigma$ at **three points**; cycle type is how Judson *finds* those three points, not a
hypothesis the algebra consumes. So the theorems are stated over point-values and distinctness,
which a caller discharges from its own decomposition, and three of them cover all five cases:

  - `theCommutatorOfAThreeCycleWithAPermutation` gives the length-free closed form
    $\sigma(i,j,a)\sigma^{-1}(i,j,a)^{-1} = T(\sigma i,\sigma a)\,T(\sigma i,\sigma j)\,T(i,j)\,T(i,a)$,
    depending only on $\sigma$'s action at the three points. This is Judson's long-cycle case
    with $r$ eliminated — he conjugates by a cycle of length $r$ and gets the $r$-dependent
    $(a_1,a_3,a_r)$; the commutator never sees $r$.
  - `squaringAThreeCycleTimesAnInvolutionGivesAThreeCycle` handles
    $\sigma = \tau(a_1a_2a_3)$ with $\tau$ an involution, via
    `squaringKillsACommutingInvolution` (pure composition algebra: if the factors commute and
    the first is an involution, $(\tau g)^2 = g^2$).
  - `theCommutatorWithASwapIsAThreeCycle` handles the all-2-cycles case. Judson conjugates
    twice there, and the first conjugation genuinely does not finish — it returns another
    double transposition. What the second one needs is only that $\sigma$ **swaps two of the
    3-cycle's points and fixes the third**: then the commutator is that 3-cycle itself. Against
    $(a_1a_3)(a_2a_4)$ the chosen 3-cycle is $(a_1,a_3,a_5)$, and the fifth point is where
    $n \geq 5$ is spent.

  The two-disjoint-3-cycles case needs no theorem of its own: conjugating by $(a_1a_2a_4)$
  builds a 5-cycle, and the first form above applies to that directly, because it reads three
  points and never a length.

  What remains for Lemma 10.10 is the bookkeeping that selects the points — walking
  `aNonIdentityDecompositionPeelsOffALastCycle` (`std/permutation/decomposition.b4m`) to supply
  each case's point-values — not the cycle arithmetic. `CHAPTER10-PAINPOINTS` records that four
  successive "missing machinery" claims about this lemma were each disproved by a probe.

**Lemma 10.9 is now complete**, and so is the composition that turns it into Theorem 10.11. The
pieces, all strict and hole-free in `std/permutation/alternating-identities.b4m`:

- **Reachability.** One conjugation carries $(i,j,a)$ to $(i,j,k)$ for any $k$ outside
  $\{i,j\}$, so the whole fixed family follows from one member. The conjugator is the 3-cycle
  $(a,b,c)$, even by construction.
- **The selector.** Every 3-cycle on distinct points is a product of family members. This is a
  $4 \times 4$ case tree on where the fixed pair sits among the 3-cycle's three points — 13
  real leaves, each closing by one of Judson's four identities, plus three arms that close by
  contradiction. 844 generated lines, and the generator is kept in
  `drafts/selector-generator/` so it is regenerable.
- **The bridge.** `isAThreeCycle` is a nested existential, so "every distinct triple" and
  "every 3-cycle" are the same statement; the conversion is its own lemma because every
  consumer needs it.
- **Point supply.** $n \geq 5$ is stated as `set_finite.aSetOfAtLeastFiveHasAFifthPoint` — a set
  of size $\geq 5$ has a member outside any four named ones. It is needed because `Element` is
  an unconstrained sort, so "five points exist" has no expression as a fact about elements.

**Historical note.** Galois was the first to prove $A_5$ simple. Classifying all finite
simple groups took until the late twentieth century; Feit and Thompson's 1963 proof of
Burnside's conjecture (that every nonabelian finite simple group has even order) ran
over 250 pages and set that program in motion.
