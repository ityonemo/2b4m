Suppose we wish to show that $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ for any natural
number $n$. This formula is easily verified for small numbers such as $n = 1$, $2$, $3$,
or $4$, but it is impossible to verify for all natural numbers on a case-by-case basis.
To prove the formula true in general, a more generic method is required.

Suppose we have verified the equation for the first $n$ cases. We will attempt to show
that we can generate the formula for the $(n+1)$th case from this knowledge. The formula
is true for $n = 1$ since $1 = \frac{1(1+1)}{2}$. If we have verified the first $n$
cases, then
$$\begin{aligned}
1 + 2 + \cdots + n + (n+1) &= \frac{n(n+1)}{2} + n + 1 \\
                            &= \frac{n^2 + 3n + 2}{2} \\
                            &= \frac{(n+1)[(n+1)+1]}{2}.
\end{aligned}$$
This is exactly the formula for the $(n+1)$th case.

This method of proof is known as **mathematical induction**. Instead of attempting to
verify a statement about some subset $S$ of the positive integers $\mathbb{N}$ on a
case-by-case basis, an impossible task if $S$ is an infinite set, we give a specific proof
for the smallest integer being considered, followed by a generic argument showing that if
the statement holds for a given case, then it must also hold for the next case in the
sequence. We summarize mathematical induction in the following axiom.

## Mathematical Induction

> **First Principle of Mathematical Induction.** Let $S(n)$ be a statement about
> integers for $n \in \mathbb N$ and suppose $S(n_0)$ is true for some integer
> $n_0$. If for all integers $k \ge n_0$, $S(k)$ implies $S(k+1)$, then $S(n)$ is
> true for all integers $n \ge n_0$.

> *Judson states the first principle on $\mathbb Z$ at an arbitrary base point
> $n_0$; we are instead taking it over the nonnegatives $\mathbb N$ at
> $n_0 = 0$ — prove $P(0)$ and that $P(k)$ implies $P(k+1)$ for nonnegative $k$,
> and $P$ holds on all of $\mathbb N$ — which after a shift is what the examples
> below use.*

```2b4m
import nonneg <<< "std/integer.b4m"

pred is_nonneg = nonneg.nonneg
fact nonnegInduction = nonneg.nonnegInduction
```

> *Judson's divisibility example is "$9$ divides $10^{n+1} + 3\cdot 10^n + 5$", whose
> induction starts at $n = 1$; we are proving **$3$ divides $4^n - 1$** instead
> (equivalently $4^n \equiv 1 \pmod 3$), which is the same shape with the induction
> starting at $0$. His own example is Exercise 5 in the exercises file.*

This is the chapter's first use of *divisibility* and of *powers*.

```2b4m
import divides <<< "std/integer.b4m"

func pow = divides.pow
define does_divide(d, n) = divides.divides(d, n)
fact powZero = divides.powZero
fact powSucc = divides.powSucc
fact dividesRefl = divides.dividesRefl
fact dividesMul = divides.dividesMul
fact dividesAdd = divides.dividesAdd
```

At $n = 0$: $4^0 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0$, and $3 \mid 0$.

```2b4m
theorem baseCase: does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE))
proof
  @four-to-zero |
    pow(FOUR, ZERO) = ONE
    [using specialize powZero(FOUR)]
  @reflexivity-of-difference |
    sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE) = sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE)
    [by reflexivity]
  @difference-of-ones |
    sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE) = sub(ONE, ONE)
    [by rewrite four-to-zero reflexivity-of-difference]
  @one-minus-one |
    sub(ONE, ONE) = ZERO
    [using arithmetic]
  @difference-is-zero |
    sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE) = ZERO
    [by rewrite one-minus-one difference-of-ones]
  @zero-as-product |
    ZERO = mul(THREE, ZERO)
    [using arithmetic]
  @three-divides-zero |
    does_divide(THREE, ZERO)
    [by witness(ZERO) zero-as-product]
  @conclusion |
    does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE))
    [by rewrite difference-is-zero three-divides-zero]
qed
```

The inductive step rests on the algebraic identity
$4^{k+1} - 1 = 4(4^k - 1) + 3$: multiplying the difference $4^k - 1$ by $4$ and
adding $3$ recovers the next difference $4^{k+1} - 1$. Written with $x = 4^k$
that is $(x-1)\cdot 4 + 3 = x \cdot 4 - 1$.

```2b4m
theorem successorDifferenceIdentity: forall x: Int;
  add(mul(sub(x, ONE), FOUR), THREE) = sub(mul(x, FOUR), ONE)
proof
  @generalize-x |
    fix x: Int {
      @conclusion-difference-splits-off-three |
        add(mul(sub(x, ONE), FOUR), THREE) = sub(mul(x, FOUR), ONE)
        [using simplify definitionOfSubtraction mulZeroRight mulSuccRight addZeroRight addZeroLeft addSuccRight addPrevRight addSuccLeft addPrevLeft negSucc negZero oneIsSuccZero succPrev prevSucc addIsAssociative]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall x: Int; add(mul(sub(x, ONE), FOUR), THREE) = sub(mul(x, FOUR), ONE)
    [by generalize generalize-x]
qed
```

Now induction closes the theorem. For the step, assume $3 \mid 4^k - 1$; then
$3 \mid 4(4^k-1)$, being a multiple of a multiple of $3$, and $3 \mid 3$, so $3$
divides their sum $4(4^k-1)+3$, which by the identity above is $4^{k+1}-1$.

```2b4m
theorem fourthPowerMinusOne:
  forall n: Int; is_nonneg(n) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, n), ONE))
proof
  @induction-step |
    fix k: Int {
      @given-inductive-hypothesis |
        assume does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE)) {
          @inductive-hypothesis |
            does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE))
            [by hypothesis given-inductive-hypothesis]
          @three-divides-difference-times-four |
            does_divide(THREE, mul(sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE), FOUR))
            [using specialize dividesMul(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE), FOUR) inductive-hypothesis]
          @three-divides-three |
            does_divide(THREE, THREE)
            [using specialize dividesRefl(THREE)]
          @three-divides-sum |
            does_divide(THREE, add(mul(sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE), FOUR), THREE))
            [using specialize dividesAdd(THREE, mul(sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE), FOUR), THREE) three-divides-difference-times-four three-divides-three]
          @sum-splits-the-successor-difference |
            add(mul(sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE), FOUR), THREE) = sub(mul(pow(FOUR, k), FOUR), ONE)
            [using specialize successorDifferenceIdentity(pow(FOUR, k))]
          @three-divides-successor-power-product |
            does_divide(THREE, sub(mul(pow(FOUR, k), FOUR), ONE))
            [by rewrite sum-splits-the-successor-difference three-divides-sum]
          @successor-power-unfolds |
            pow(FOUR, succ(k)) = mul(pow(FOUR, k), FOUR)
            [using specialize powSucc(FOUR, k)]
          @three-divides-successor-difference |
            does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, succ(k)), ONE))
            [by rewrite successor-power-unfolds three-divides-successor-power-product]
        }
      @inductive-hypothesis-implies-successor |
        does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE)) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, succ(k)), ONE))
        [by discharge given-inductive-hypothesis]
      @induction-step-at-k |
        is_nonneg(k) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE)) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, succ(k)), ONE))
        [using tautology inductive-hypothesis-implies-successor]
    }
  @induction-step-for-all-k |
    forall k: Int; is_nonneg(k) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, k), ONE)) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, succ(k)), ONE))
    [by generalize induction-step]
  @base-case |
    does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, ZERO), ONE))
    [by cite baseCase]
  @conclusion |
    forall n: Int; is_nonneg(n) -> does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, n), ONE))
    [using instantiation nonnegInduction((fun m: Int => does_divide(THREE, sub(pow(FOUR, m), ONE)))) base-case induction-step-for-all-k]
qed
```

### The Second Principle and Well-Ordering

> **Second Principle of Mathematical Induction.** Let $S(n)$ be a statement about
> integers for $n \in \mathbb N$ and suppose $S(n_0)$ is true for some integer
> $n_0$. If $S(n_0), S(n_0+1), \ldots, S(k)$ imply $S(k+1)$ for $k \ge n_0$, then
> $S(n)$ is true for all integers $n \ge n_0$.

We have an equivalent statement of the Principle of Mathematical Induction that
is often very useful. The second principle is *strong*, or course-of-values,
induction: rather than $S(k)$ alone, the step may assume every earlier case. It
and the Principle of Well-Ordering below are both consequences of the first
principle, and are proved from it here.

```2b4m
import wellordering <<< "std/integer.b4m"

pred less_than = order.less_than
pred at_most = order.less_or_equal
fact nonnegStrongInduction = wellordering.nonnegStrongInduction
fact nonnegWellOrdering = wellordering.nonnegWellOrdering
```

> A nonempty subset $S$ of $\mathbb Z$ is **well-ordered** if $S$ contains a least
> element. The integers are not well-ordered — they have no smallest element — but
> the natural numbers are.
>
> **Principle of Well-Ordering.** Every nonempty subset of the natural numbers
> contains a least element.

The Principle of Well-Ordering is equivalent to the Principle of Mathematical
Induction. Here is the implication in the direction the division algorithm will
need: assume a nonempty subset of $\mathbb N$ has *no* least element; then by
strong induction no natural number belongs to it, so it is empty — contradicting
nonemptiness.

> *Judson quantifies over subsets $S \subset \mathbb{N}$; there is no sort of
> subsets here, so a subset is instead named by its membership predicate, and the
> principle is a schema in that predicate.*
