A literate `bpa` transliteration of **Chapter 4 ("Cyclic Groups"), §4.2 "The
Multiplicative Group of Complex Numbers"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract
Algebra: Theory and Applications* (source: `vendor/aata/src/cyclic.xml`; ©
1997–2026 Judson, GFDL 1.3). The book's prose is reproduced in order. The
**algebraic** heart — complex arithmetic, the conjugate, and the modulus with its
multiplicative law — is formalized with machine-checked proofs; the **analytic**

## Complex arithmetic

The **complex numbers** are defined as
$$\mathbb{C} = \{ a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{R} \},$$
where $i^2 = -1$. If $z = a + bi$, then $a$ is the **real part** of $z$ and $b$
is the **imaginary part**. To add, we add corresponding parts:
$$z + w = (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i;$$
and remembering $i^2 = -1$, we multiply like polynomials:
$$(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.$$
Every nonzero $z = a + bi$ has a multiplicative inverse
$$z^{-1} = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2},$$
so $\mathbb{C}^* = \mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ is a group under multiplication.
The **complex conjugate** of $z = a + bi$ is $\overline{z} = a - bi$, and the
**absolute value** or **modulus** is $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

These are exactly the field structure of `std/complex.bpa` ($i^2 = -1$, the ℝ
embedding, `re`/`im`/`conj`) together with the modulus of
`std/complex/modulus.bpa`; the group $\mathbb{C}^*$ is that field's nonzero
elements under multiplication.

## The modulus and its properties

The key structural facts about the modulus — the ones §4.2 uses and Exercise 19
asks to prove — are that $z\overline{z} = |z|^2$, that conjugation preserves the
modulus, and that the modulus is **multiplicative**, $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$. The last
is what makes the circle group $\mathbb{T} = \{ z : |z| = 1 \}$ a group. Each is
proved in `std/complex/modulus.bpa` (from the projection algebra of ℂ, with no
trigonometry); we state them here in the book's notation.

$z \overline{z} = |z|^2$ (as an embedded real, $|z|^2 = \mathrm{re}(z)^2 +
\mathrm{im}(z)^2$):

```bpa
theorem conjugateProduct: forall z: C; mul(z, conj(z)) = complex.fromReal(normSq(z))
proof
  @conclusion |
    forall z: C; mul(z, conj(z)) = complex.fromReal(normSq(z))
    [using import(complex_modulus) zTimesConjIsNormSq]
qed
```

Conjugation preserves the modulus, $|\overline{z}| = |z|$ (Exercise 19a):

```bpa
theorem modulusOfConjugate: forall z: C; abs(conj(z)) = abs(z)
proof
  @conclusion |
    forall z: C; abs(conj(z)) = abs(z)
    [using import(complex_modulus) absConj]
qed
```

The modulus is multiplicative, $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$ (Exercise 19f):

```bpa
theorem modulusMultiplicative: forall z, w: C; abs(mul(z, w)) = real.mul(abs(z), abs(w))
proof
  @conclusion |
    forall z, w: C; abs(mul(z, w)) = real.mul(abs(z), abs(w))
    [using import(complex_modulus) absMul]
qed
```

## Polar form and DeMoivre's theorem

The polar representation of a complex number makes products and powers easy.
Writing $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\operatorname{cis}\theta$ with $r =
|z|$, one has, for $z = r\operatorname{cis}\theta$ and $w =
s\operatorname{cis}\phi$,
$$zw = rs\operatorname{cis}(\theta + \phi),$$
and **DeMoivre's Theorem**: for a nonzero $z = r\operatorname{cis}\theta$,
$$[r\operatorname{cis}\theta]^n = r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta), \quad n = 1, 2,
\ldots,$$
proved by induction on $n$ using the angle-addition formulas for sine and cosine.

## The circle group and the roots of unity

The circle group is
$$\mathbb{T} = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1 \},$$
a subgroup of $\mathbb{C}^*$ (a direct consequence of $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$, proved
above). Although $\mathbb{T}$ has infinite order it has many finite subgroups.
The complex numbers satisfying $z^n = 1$ are the **$n$th roots of unity**; by
DeMoivre's theorem they are
$$z = \operatorname{cis}\!\left(\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right), \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n
- 1,$$
and they form a cyclic subgroup of $\mathbb{T}$ of order $n$. A generator is a
**primitive $n$th root of unity**.

The circle group $\mathbb{T}$ itself — the predicate $|z| = 1$ — is expressible
once the modulus is in hand (it is defined above), and "$\mathbb{T}$ is a
subgroup" follows from `modulusMultiplicative` together with $|1| = 1$ and
$|z^{-1}| = |z|^{-1}$. But its finite subgroups, the roots of unity, are stated
via $\operatorname{cis}$, so they inherit the trigonometry gap:
