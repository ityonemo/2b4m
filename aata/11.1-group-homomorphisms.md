## The definition

**Definition.** A **homomorphism** of groups between groups $(G, \cdot)$ and
$(H, \circ)$ is a map $\phi : G \rightarrow H$ such that
$$\phi( g_1 \cdot g_2 ) = \phi( g_1 ) \circ \phi( g_2 )$$
for $g_1, g_2 \in G$. The range of $\phi$ in $H$ is called the **homomorphic image**
of $\phi$.

Two groups are related in the strongest possible way if they are isomorphic; however, a
weaker relationship may exist between two groups. For example, the symmetric group $S_n$
and the group $\mathbb{Z}_2$ are related by the fact that $S_n$ can be divided into even
and odd permutations that exhibit a group structure like that of $\mathbb{Z}_2$:
$$\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & \text{even} & \text{odd} \\ \hline
\text{even} & \text{even} & \text{odd} \\
\text{odd} & \text{odd} & \text{even}
\end{array}$$
We use homomorphisms to study relationships such as the one we have just described.

**Example.** Let $G$ be a group and $g \in G$. Define $\phi : \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow G$
by $\phi(n) = g^n$. Then $\phi$ is a group homomorphism, since
$\phi(m+n) = g^{m+n} = g^m g^n = \phi(m)\phi(n)$. This homomorphism maps $\mathbb{Z}$
onto the cyclic subgroup of $G$ generated by $g$.

**Example.** Let $G = GL_2(\mathbb{R})$. For $A \in G$ the determinant is nonzero, and
$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ for any two elements $A, B$ in $G$; so $A \mapsto \det(A)$
is a homomorphism $GL_2(\mathbb{R}) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^\ast$.

**Example.** The circle group $\mathbb{T}$ consists of all complex numbers $z$ with
$|z| = 1$. The map $\phi : \theta \mapsto \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is a homomorphism
from the additive group $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{T}$, since the angle-addition formulas
give
$$\phi(\alpha + \beta) = (\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta)
= \phi(\alpha)\phi(\beta).$$
Geometrically, we are wrapping the real line around the circle in a group-theoretic
fashion.

> *Our theory of maps acts on one fixed element sort, so $\phi$ is declared as a
> primitive function rather than borrowed from it: a homomorphism's source and target
> differ by construction.*

## What a homomorphism preserves

**Proposition 11.4.** Let $\phi : G_1 \rightarrow G_2$ be a homomorphism of groups.
Then

1. If $e$ is the identity of $G_1$, then $\phi(e)$ is the identity of $G_2$;
2. For any element $g \in G_1$, $\phi(g^{-1}) = [\phi(g)]^{-1}$;
3. If $H_1$ is a subgroup of $G_1$, then $\phi(H_1)$ is a subgroup of $G_2$;
4. If $H_2$ is a subgroup of $G_2$, then $\phi^{-1}(H_2) = \{g \in G_1 : \phi(g) \in H_2\}$
   is a subgroup of $G_1$. Furthermore, if $H_2$ is normal in $G_2$, then
   $\phi^{-1}(H_2)$ is normal in $G_1$.

**Proof.** (1) Suppose that $e$ and $e'$ are the identities of $G_1$ and $G_2$;
then $e'\phi(e) = \phi(e) = \phi(ee) = \phi(e)\phi(e)$. By cancellation,
$\phi(e) = e'$. (2) This follows from
$\phi(g^{-1})\phi(g) = \phi(g^{-1}g) = \phi(e) = e'$. (3) The set $\phi(H_1)$ is
nonempty since the identity of $G_2$ is in it; and
$xy^{-1} = \phi(a)[\phi(b)]^{-1} = \phi(ab^{-1}) \in \phi(H_1)$. (4) The identity is in
$H_1$ since $\phi(e) = e'$; if $a, b \in H_1$ then
$\phi(ab^{-1}) = \phi(a)[\phi(b)]^{-1}$ is in $H_2$. $\blacksquare$

Parts (1) and (2) both run on cancellation in $G_2$.

```2b4m
theorem phiPreservesIdentity: phi(EG) = EH
proof
  @identity-is-idempotent |
    opG(EG, EG) = EG
    [using specialize opGIdentityLeft(EG)]
  @phi-of-the-product |
    phi(opG(EG, EG)) = opH(phi(EG), phi(EG))
    [using specialize phiPreservesOp(EG, EG)]
  @phi-of-the-identity |
    phi(EG) = opH(phi(EG), phi(EG))
    [by rewrite identity-is-idempotent phi-of-the-product]
  @the-identity-absorbs |
    opH(EH, phi(EG)) = phi(EG)
    [using specialize opHIdentityLeft(phi(EG))]
  @both-products-agree |
    opH(EH, phi(EG)) = opH(phi(EG), phi(EG))
    [using chain the-identity-absorbs phi-of-the-identity]
  @the-identity-is-the-image |
    EH = phi(EG)
    [using specialize cancelRightH(EH, phi(EG), phi(EG)) both-products-agree]
  @conclusion |
    phi(EG) = EH
    [by symmetry the-identity-is-the-image]
qed

theorem phiPreservesInverses: forall g: G; phi(invG(g)) = invH(phi(g))
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: G {
      @the-inverse-cancels |
        opG(invG(g), g) = EG
        [using specialize opGInverseLeft(g)]
      @phi-of-the-product |
        phi(opG(invG(g), g)) = opH(phi(invG(g)), phi(g))
        [using specialize phiPreservesOp(invG(g), g)]
      @phi-of-the-identity-product |
        phi(EG) = opH(phi(invG(g)), phi(g))
        [by rewrite the-inverse-cancels phi-of-the-product]
      @the-image-of-the-identity |
        phi(EG) = EH
        [by cite phiPreservesIdentity]
      @the-product-is-the-identity |
        EH = opH(phi(invG(g)), phi(g))
        [by rewrite the-image-of-the-identity phi-of-the-identity-product]
      @the-inverses-product |
        opH(invH(phi(g)), phi(g)) = EH
        [using specialize opHInverseLeft(phi(g))]
      @both-agree |
        opH(invH(phi(g)), phi(g)) = opH(phi(invG(g)), phi(g))
        [using chain the-inverses-product the-product-is-the-identity]
      @the-inverse-is-the-image |
        invH(phi(g)) = phi(invG(g))
        [using specialize cancelRightH(invH(phi(g)), phi(g), phi(invG(g))) both-agree]
      @conclusion-at-g |
        phi(invG(g)) = invH(phi(g))
        [by symmetry the-inverse-is-the-image]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g: G; phi(invG(g)) = invH(phi(g))
    [by generalize generalize-g]
qed
```

For (3) and (4), each "is a subgroup" claim is the three closure conditions on the
image $\phi(H_1)$ and on the preimage $\phi^{-1}(H_2)$ respectively, plus — when $H_2$
is normal — that $g^{-1}hg$ lands in the preimage, since
$\phi(g^{-1}hg) = [\phi(g)]^{-1}\phi(h)\phi(g) \in H_2$.

> *With no sort of subgroups to quantify over, $H_1$ and $H_2$ are carried by membership
> predicates on $G$ and on $H$.*

```2b4m
pred inSource = homomorphism.inSource
pred inTarget = homomorphism.inTarget
define inImage(x) = homomorphism.inImage(x)
define inPreimage(g) = homomorphism.inPreimage(g)

// (3) phi(H_1) is a subgroup of G_2
theorem theImageHasTheIdentity: inImage(EH)
proof
  @the-identity-is-in-the-source |
    inSource(EG)
    [by cite sourceHasIdentity]
  @the-image-of-the-identity |
    phi(EG) = EH
    [by cite phiPreservesIdentity]
  @the-identity-is-its-own-image |
    EH = phi(EG)
    [by symmetry the-image-of-the-identity]
  @the-witness |
    inSource(EG) and EH = phi(EG)
    [by both the-identity-is-in-the-source the-identity-is-its-own-image]
  @conclusion |
    inImage(EH)
    [by witness(EG) the-witness]
qed

theorem theImageIsClosedUnderTheOperation: forall x: H; forall y: H;
  inImage(x) -> inImage(y) -> inImage(opH(x, y))
proof
  @generalize-x |
    fix x: H {
      @generalize-y |
        fix y: H {
          @given-x-in-the-image |
            assume inImage(x) {
              @given-y-in-the-image |
                assume inImage(y) {

                    [by hypothesis given-y-in-the-image]
                  @with-x-preimage |
                    unpack a: G from x-is-an-image {
                      @the-x-witness |
                        inSource(a) and x = phi(a)
                        [by hypothesis with-x-preimage]

                        inSource(a)
                        [by and_lhs the-x-witness]
                      @x-is-phi-a |
                        x = phi(a)
                        [by and_rhs the-x-witness]
                      @with-y-preimage |
                        unpack b: G from y-is-an-image {
                          @the-y-witness |

                            [by hypothesis with-y-preimage]

                          @the-product-of-images |

                            inImage(opH(x, y))
                            [by witness(opG(a, b)) the-witness]
                        }
```

## The kernel

**Definition.** Let $\phi : G \rightarrow H$ be a group homomorphism and suppose that
$e$ is the identity of $H$. By Proposition 11.4, $\phi^{-1}(\{e\})$ is a subgroup of
$G$. This subgroup is called the **kernel** of $\phi$, denoted $\ker \phi$.

**Theorem 11.6.** Let $\phi : G \rightarrow H$ be a group homomorphism. Then the kernel
of $\phi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.

**Proof.** This subgroup is a normal subgroup of $G$ since the trivial subgroup is
normal in $H$ — it is Proposition 11.4(4) at $H_2 = \{e'\}$, the kernel being exactly
$\phi^{-1}(\{e'\})$. $\blacksquare$

Taken directly, without a subgroup predicate on $H$:

```2b4m
pred inKernel = homomorphism.inKernel
fact inKernelDef = homomorphism.inKernelDef

theorem theKernelHasTheIdentity: inKernel(EG)
proof
  @the-image-of-the-identity |
    phi(EG) = EH
    [by cite phiPreservesIdentity]
  @what-the-kernel-means |
    inKernel(EG) iff phi(EG) = EH
    [using specialize inKernelDef(EG)]
  @conclusion |
    inKernel(EG)
    [using tautology what-the-kernel-means the-image-of-the-identity]
qed

                    [by hypothesis given-b-in-the-kernel]

                    [using specialize inKernelDef(a)]

                    [using specialize inKernelDef(opG(a, b))]
                  @conclusion-the-product-is-in-the-kernel |
                    inKernel(opG(a, b))
                    [using tautology what-the-kernel-means-for-the-product the-product-is-killed]
                }
              @b-in-kernel-implies |
                inKernel(b) -> inKernel(opG(a, b))
                [by discharge given-b-in-the-kernel]
            }
          @conclusion-at-a-and-b |
            inKernel(a) -> inKernel(b) -> inKernel(opG(a, b))
            [by discharge given-a-in-the-kernel]
        }
      @discharge-b |

    }
  @conclusion |
    forall a: G; forall b: G; inKernel(a) -> inKernel(b) -> inKernel(opG(a, b))
    [by generalize generalize-a]
qed

theorem theKernelIsNormal: forall g: G; forall n: G;
  inKernel(n) -> inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))
proof
  @generalize-g |
    fix g: G {
      @generalize-n |
        fix n: G {
          @given-n-in-the-kernel |
            assume inKernel(n) {
              @n-is-in-the-kernel |
                inKernel(n)
                [by hypothesis given-n-in-the-kernel]
              @what-the-kernel-means-for-n |
                inKernel(n) iff phi(n) = EH
                [using specialize inKernelDef(n)]
              @phi-kills-n |
                phi(n) = EH
                [using tautology what-the-kernel-means-for-n n-is-in-the-kernel]

              // phi(gng^-1) = phi(gn) phi(g^-1) = phi(g)phi(n) phi(g)^-1
              @split-the-outer |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = opH(phi(opG(g, n)), phi(invG(g)))
                [using specialize phiPreservesOp(opG(g, n), invG(g))]
              @split-the-inner |
                phi(opG(g, n)) = opH(phi(g), phi(n))
                [using specialize phiPreservesOp(g, n)]
              @with-the-inner-split |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = opH(opH(phi(g), phi(n)), phi(invG(g)))
                [by rewrite split-the-inner split-the-outer]
              @the-image-of-the-inverse |
                phi(invG(g)) = invH(phi(g))
                [using specialize phiPreservesInverses(g)]
              @with-the-inverse |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = opH(opH(phi(g), phi(n)), invH(phi(g)))
                [by rewrite the-image-of-the-inverse with-the-inner-split]
              @with-n-killed |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = opH(opH(phi(g), EH), invH(phi(g)))
                [by rewrite phi-kills-n with-the-inverse]

              // phi(g) e' phi(g)^-1 = phi(g) phi(g)^-1 = e'
              @the-identity-absorbs-on-the-right |
                opH(phi(g), EH) = phi(g)
                [using specialize identityRightH(phi(g))]
              @collapsed |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = opH(phi(g), invH(phi(g)))
                [by rewrite the-identity-absorbs-on-the-right with-n-killed]
              @the-inverse-cancels-on-the-right |
                opH(phi(g), invH(phi(g))) = EH
                [using specialize inverseRightH(phi(g))]
              @the-conjugate-is-killed |
                phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = EH
                [using chain collapsed the-inverse-cancels-on-the-right]
              @what-the-kernel-means-for-the-conjugate |
                inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) iff phi(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g))) = EH
                [using specialize inKernelDef(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))]
              @conclusion-the-conjugate-is-in |
                inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))
                [using tautology what-the-kernel-means-for-the-conjugate the-conjugate-is-killed]
            }
          @conclusion-at-n |
            inKernel(n) -> inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))
            [by discharge given-n-in-the-kernel]
        }
      @discharge-n |
        forall n: G; inKernel(n) -> inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))
        [by generalize generalize-n]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall g: G; forall n: G; inKernel(n) -> inKernel(opG(opG(g, n), invG(g)))
    [by generalize generalize-g]
qed
```

## A homomorphism is injective exactly when its kernel is trivial

$\phi$ is one-to-one exactly when $\ker\phi = \{e\}$: if $\phi(a) = \phi(b)$ then
$\phi(a^{-1}b) = e'$, so $a^{-1}b$ is in the kernel and $a = b$; conversely a one-to-one
$\phi$ sends only $e$ to $e'$. §11.2 reads the First Isomorphism Theorem's last step
"$\ker\psi = \{e\}$" as "$\psi$ is one-to-one" this way, and it is how the
$\mathbb{Z}_7 \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}_{12}$ example below concludes that no injective
homomorphism exists.

```2b4m
theorem aTrivialKernelMakesPhiInjective:
  (forall g: G; inKernel(g) -> g = EG) ->
  forall a: G; forall b: G; phi(a) = phi(b) -> a = b
proof
  @given-the-kernel-is-trivial |
    assume forall g: G; inKernel(g) -> g = EG {
      @the-kernel-is-trivial |
        forall g: G; inKernel(g) -> g = EG
        [by hypothesis given-the-kernel-is-trivial]
      @generalize-a |
        fix a: G {
          @generalize-b |
            fix b: G {
              @given-images-agree |
                assume phi(a) = phi(b) {
                  @images-agree |
                    phi(a) = phi(b)
                    [by hypothesis given-images-agree]
                  // φ(a⁻¹b) = φ(a)⁻¹φ(b) = φ(a)⁻¹φ(a) = e'
                  @phi-splits-the-quotient |
                    phi(opG(invG(a), b)) = opH(phi(invG(a)), phi(b))
                    [using specialize phiPreservesOp(invG(a), b)]
                  @phi-of-the-inverse |
                    phi(invG(a)) = invH(phi(a))
                    [using specialize phiPreservesInverses(a)]
                  @the-quotients-image |
                    phi(opG(invG(a), b)) = opH(invH(phi(a)), phi(b))
                    [by rewrite phi-of-the-inverse phi-splits-the-quotient]
                  @with-the-images-equal |
                    phi(opG(invG(a), b)) = opH(invH(phi(a)), phi(a))
                    [by rewrite images-agree the-quotients-image]
                  @the-inverse-cancels |
                    opH(invH(phi(a)), phi(a)) = EH
                    [using specialize opHInverseLeft(phi(a))]
                  @the-quotient-is-killed |
                    phi(opG(invG(a), b)) = EH
                    [using chain with-the-images-equal the-inverse-cancels]

                    [using specialize inKernelDef(opG(invG(a), b))]

                    [by symmetry the-regrouping]

                    [using specialize inverseRightG(a)]
                  @the-left-side-is-b |
                    opG(opG(a, invG(a)), b) = opG(EG, b)
                    [using simplify the-inverse-cancels-in-g]
                  @the-identity-absorbs |
                    opG(EG, b) = b
                    [using specialize opGIdentityLeft(b)]
                  @the-regrouped-left-is-b |
                    opG(a, opG(invG(a), b)) = b
                    [using chain the-left-side-regroups the-left-side-is-b the-identity-absorbs]
                  @the-right-side-is-a |
                    opG(a, EG) = a
                    [using specialize identityRightG(a)]
                  @b-is-a |
                    b = a
                    [using chain the-regrouped-left-is-b a-times-the-quotient the-right-side-is-a]

                    [by symmetry b-is-a]

      forall a: G; forall b: G; phi(a) = phi(b) -> a = b
```

## Kernels of the examples

**Example.** For $\phi : GL_2(\mathbb{R}) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^\ast$ given by
$A \mapsto \det(A)$, the identity of $\mathbb{R}^\ast$ is 1, so the kernel is all
$2 \times 2$ matrices of determinant one: $\ker\phi = SL_2(\mathbb{R})$.

**Example.** The kernel of $\phi : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}^\ast$ given by
$\phi(\theta) = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is $\{2\pi n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$; notice
that $\ker\phi \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

**Example.** Suppose we wish to determine all homomorphisms $\phi$ from $\mathbb{Z}_7$
to $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$. Since $\ker\phi$ must be a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_7$, there are
only two possible kernels, $\{0\}$ and all of $\mathbb{Z}_7$. The image of a subgroup of
$\mathbb{Z}_7$ must be a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$; hence there is no injective
homomorphism, for otherwise $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ would have a subgroup of order 7, which is
impossible. Consequently the only homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}_7$ to $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$
is the one mapping every element to zero.

**Example.** Let $g \in G$ and let $\phi$ be the homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $G$
given by $\phi(n) = g^n$. If the order of $g$ is infinite, the kernel is $\{0\}$, since
$\phi$ maps $\mathbb{Z}$ onto the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$. If the order of $g$
is finite, say $n$, then the kernel of $\phi$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$.
