# Irreducible Polynomials

A literate `2b4m` transliteration of **Chapter 17 ("Polynomials"), §17.3 "Irreducible
Polynomials"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications* (source:
`vendor/aata/src/poly.xml`, lines 683–1075; © 1997–2026 Judson, GFDL 1.3). Run
`2b4m check aata/17.3-irreducible-polynomials.md` to verify.

A nonconstant polynomial $f(x) \in F[x]$ is **irreducible** over a field $F$ if $f(x)$ cannot
be expressed as a product of two polynomials $g(x)$ and $h(x)$ in $F[x]$, where the degrees of
$g(x)$ and $h(x)$ are both smaller than the degree of $f(x)$. Irreducible polynomials function
as the prime numbers of polynomial rings.

```2b4m

// Judson's definition, verbatim: nonconstant, and not a product of two strictly
// lower-degree factors.
import polyirreducible <<< "std/field/irreducible.b4m"

fact irreducibilityIsNoLowerDegreeFactorisation =
  polyirreducible.irreducibilityIsNoLowerDegreeFactorisation
```

> **A divergence from Judson: denominators carry a nonzero HYPOTHESIS.** Where he writes
> $p(x) = b_0/c_0 + \dots + b_n/c_n x^n$ and takes the $c_i \neq 0$ as understood, this
> transcription states it. 2b4m's reciprocal is a GUARDED function — `recip(a)` is not a term
> one can write without discharging `nonzero(a)` where it appears — so "the $c_i$ are nonzero"
> becomes an antecedent of every statement that divides by one, rather than a side remark. The
> mathematics is unchanged; what changes is that nothing here can divide by zero silently.

> **Numerics are not in scope.** His three examples — $x^2 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, $x^2 + 1$
> over $\mathbb{R}$, and $x^3 + x^2 + 2$ over $\mathbb{Z}_3$ (checked by evaluating at $0$,
> $1$, $2$) — are numeric verifications over specific fields, and the chapter's convention
> leaves those untranslated. The general remark they illustrate is the definition above.

A first consequence of the definition, in the form the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ example uses: an
irreducible polynomial has no factorisation into two lower-degree pieces, so exhibiting one
refutes irreducibility.

```2b4m
theorem aFactorisationRefutesIrreducibility: forall f, g, h: Poly;
  isIrreducible(f) -> f = mul(g, h) ->
  (not (less_than(degree(g), degree(f)) and less_than(degree(h), degree(f))))
proof
  @generalize-f |
    fix f: Poly {
      @generalize-g |
        fix g: Poly {
          @generalize-h |
            fix h: Poly {
              @given-irreducible |
                assume isIrreducible(f) {
                  @irreducible |
                    isIrreducible(f)
                    [by hypothesis given-irreducible]
                  @the-definition |
                    isIrreducible(f) iff
                      (less_than(ZERO, degree(f)) and
                      (forall g2, h2: Poly;
                      f = mul(g2, h2) ->
                      (not (less_than(degree(g2), degree(f)) and
                      less_than(degree(h2), degree(f))))))
                    [using specialize irreducibilityIsNoLowerDegreeFactorisation(f)]
                  @the-two-conjuncts |
                    less_than(ZERO, degree(f)) and
                      (forall g2, h2: Poly;
                      f = mul(g2, h2) ->
                      (not (less_than(degree(g2), degree(f)) and
                      less_than(degree(h2), degree(f)))))
                    [using tautology the-definition irreducible]
                  // the definition's second conjunct is the no-factorisation clause
                  @the-no-factorisation-clause |
                    forall g2, h2: Poly;
                      f = mul(g2, h2) ->
                      (not (less_than(degree(g2), degree(f)) and
                      less_than(degree(h2), degree(f))))
                    [by and_rhs the-two-conjuncts]
                  @conclusion-at-these-factors |
                    f = mul(g, h) ->
                      (not (less_than(degree(g), degree(f)) and
                      less_than(degree(h), degree(f))))
                    [using specialize the-no-factorisation-clause(g, h)]
                }
              @irreducible-implies-no-factorisation |
                isIrreducible(f) -> f = mul(g, h) ->
                  (not (less_than(degree(g), degree(f)) and
                  less_than(degree(h), degree(f))))
                [by discharge given-irreducible]
            }
          @discharge-h |
            forall h9: Poly;
              isIrreducible(f) -> f = mul(g, h9) ->
              (not (less_than(degree(g), degree(f)) and
              less_than(degree(h9), degree(f))))
            [by generalize generalize-h]
        }
      @discharge-g |
        forall g9, h9: Poly;
          isIrreducible(f) -> f = mul(g9, h9) ->
          (not (less_than(degree(g9), degree(f)) and
          less_than(degree(h9), degree(f))))
        [by generalize generalize-g]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall f, g, h: Poly;
      isIrreducible(f) -> f = mul(g, h) ->
      (not (less_than(degree(g), degree(f)) and less_than(degree(h), degree(f))))
    [by generalize generalize-f]
qed
```

**Lemma 17.12** Let $p(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$. Then
$$p(x) = \frac{r}{s}(a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n),$$
where $r, s, a_0, \ldots, a_n$ are integers, the $a_i$'s are relatively prime, and $r$ and $s$
are relatively prime.

**Proof.** Suppose that
$$p(x) = \frac{b_0}{c_0} + \frac{b_1}{c_1} x + \cdots + \frac{b_n}{c_n} x^n,$$
where the $b_i$'s and the $c_i$'s are integers. We can rewrite $p(x)$ as
$$p(x) = \frac{1}{c_0 \cdots c_n} (d_0 + d_1 x + \cdots + d_n x^n),$$
where $d_0, \ldots, d_n$ are integers. Let $d$ be the greatest common divisor of
$d_0, \ldots, d_n$. Then
$$p(x) = \frac{d}{c_0 \cdots c_n} (a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n),$$
where $d_i = d a_i$ and the $a_i$'s are relatively prime. Reducing $d /(c_0 \cdots c_n)$ to
its lowest terms, we can write
$$p(x) = \frac{r}{s}(a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_n x^n),$$
where $\gcd(r,s) = 1$. $\blacksquare$

> **$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, and divisibility stated on the cut.** `std/field/polynomial.b4m`'s
> $F[x]$ has an opaque coefficient field, so it cannot say "this coefficient is an integer".
> $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ is therefore its own sort in `std/rational/polynomial.b4m`, with $F[x]$'s
> corpus transferred onto it; what makes such a transfer work is mapping the whole
> *polynomial* layer, since a transfer re-checks the source proof and those proofs mention
> `coeff`, whose result sort is the coefficient field.
>
> Divisibility then needs nothing from `std/integer/divides.b4m`: ℚ's integer cut inherits
> ℚ's own multiplication, so "$d$ divides $n$" is an existential over the cut. And "the
> $a_i$'s are relatively prime" is a *quantification over common divisors* — every integer
> dividing all the coefficients divides $1$ — rather than a constructed $\gcd$, so it needs
> no fold either.
>
> **The construction is built, in `std/rational/integer-product.b4m`.** An earlier version of
> this note said a product over the coefficient index range "has no relational substitute" and
> that `std/sequence.b4m`'s fold could not express it. Both halves were wrong:
> `std/integer/product.b4m` has `productUpTo` with a real corpus, and `std/sequence.b4m`
> instantiates at $\mathbb{Q}$ cleanly — `sequence.Value: Q` is *unguarded*, so a
> $\mathbb{Q}$-valued product fold needs no cut relativization at all.
>
> Judson's four stages are each proved on the cut: the denominator sequence
> (`aDenominatorSequenceExists`, via a bounded index-pinned choice), the common denominator
> clearing every coefficient (`theCommonDenominatorClearsACoefficient`), dividing out the
> common factor (`theQuotientsByAMaximalDivisorAreRelativelyPrime`), and reduction to lowest
> terms (`theReducedFractionIsInLowestTerms`). The divisor's *existence* comes by descent
> rather than by constructing a $\gcd$ — well-order the first quotient
> (`aLeastFirstQuotientExists`) and a non-unit common divisor of the quotients would make it
> smaller.
>
> What that needed from $\mathbb{Q}$ itself, none of which existed: strong induction and
> well-ordering on the natural cut (`std/rational/natural-wellordering.b4m`), multiplicative
> cancellation, and a coefficient law for $\mathbb{Q}[x]$'s multiplication — `qmulP` was
> declared and mapped into $F[x]$ but *lawless*, so a product's coefficients were opaque.
>
> **PROVED.** `theIntegerDecompositionExists` concludes the decomposition's existence, on 54
> axioms with no holes. The subject polynomial is `theSubject`, an uninterpreted `func` the
> file's specs read coefficients through — a fixed but arbitrary $p$, which is what 17.12 is
> about, and the way around a schema-argument lambda carrying one shared sort annotation for
> all its binders (so `fun i: Nat, v: Q => …` could not close over a polynomial).
>
> **Two hypotheses beyond Judson's statement, both faithful to him.** The bound is
> $n+1$ because his $p$ has *degree* $n$ and so $n+1$ coefficients $a_0 \ldots a_n$, while
> every `…UpTo(_, m)` predicate means "below $m$"; `qdegreeAtMost(theSubject, n)` is then a
> real demand, because the conclusion $p = (r/s)q$ is an *unbounded* polynomial equation while
> the construction only pins coefficients below the bound — above it, his $p$ has no
> coefficients to disagree about and an arbitrary $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ element does. And
> $0 < a_0$: the divisor's existence comes by *descent*, which well-orders the first quotient
> and so needs a natural number to descend on, where Judson takes the gcd of the cleared
> numerators and a gcd needs no sign.

**Theorem 17.13 (Gauss's Lemma)** Let $p(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be a monic polynomial such that
$p(x)$ factors into a product of two polynomials $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x)$ in
$\mathbb{Q}[x]$, where the degrees of both $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x)$ are less than the degree
of $p(x)$. Then $p(x) = a(x) b(x)$, where $a(x)$ and $b(x)$ are monic polynomials in
$\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $\deg \alpha(x) = \deg a(x)$ and $\deg \beta(x) = \deg b(x)$.

**Proof.** By Lemma 17.12, we can assume that
$$\alpha(x) = \frac{c_1}{d_1}(a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_m x^m) = \frac{c_1}{d_1}\alpha_1(x)$$
$$\beta(x) = \frac{c_2}{d_2}(b_0 + b_1 x + \cdots + b_n x^n) = \frac{c_2}{d_2}\beta_1(x),$$
where the $a_i$'s are relatively prime and the $b_i$'s are relatively prime. Consequently,
$$p(x) = \alpha(x)\beta(x) = \frac{c_1 c_2}{d_1 d_2}\alpha_1(x)\beta_1(x) = \frac{c}{d}\alpha_1(x)\beta_1(x),$$
where $c/d$ is the product of $c_1/d_1$ and $c_2/d_2$ expressed in lowest terms. Hence,
$d p(x) = c \alpha_1(x) \beta_1(x)$.

If $d = 1$, then $c a_m b_n = 1$ since $p(x)$ is a monic polynomial. Hence, either $c = 1$ or
$c = -1$. If $c = 1$, then either $a_m = b_n = 1$ or $a_m = b_n = -1$. In the first case
$p(x) = \alpha_1(x)\beta_1(x)$, where $\alpha_1(x)$ and $\beta_1(x)$ are monic polynomials with
$\deg \alpha(x) = \deg \alpha_1(x)$ and $\deg \beta(x) = \deg \beta_1(x)$. In the second case
$a(x) = -\alpha_1(x)$ and $b(x) = -\beta_1(x)$ are the correct monic polynomials since
$p(x) = (-\alpha_1(x))(-\beta_1(x)) = a(x) b(x)$. The case in which $c = -1$ can be handled
similarly.

Now suppose that $d \neq 1$. Since $\gcd(c, d) = 1$, there exists a prime $p$ such that
$p \mid d$ and $p \nmid c$. Also, since the coefficients of $\alpha_1(x)$ are relatively
prime, there exists a coefficient $a_i$ such that $p \nmid a_i$. Similarly, there exists a
coefficient $b_j$ of $\beta_1(x)$ such that $p \nmid b_j$. Let $\alpha_1'(x)$ and
$\beta_1'(x)$ be the polynomials in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ obtained by reducing the coefficients of
$\alpha_1(x)$ and $\beta_1(x)$ modulo $p$. Since $p \mid d$,
$\alpha_1'(x)\beta_1'(x) = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$. However, this is impossible since neither
$\alpha_1'(x)$ nor $\beta_1'(x)$ is the zero polynomial and $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ is an integral
domain. Therefore, $d = 1$ and the theorem is proven. $\blacksquare$

> The second paragraph — the one that does the work — is encoded in full. $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and
> its coefficient reduction into $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ are `std/integer/polynomial.b4m`; the
> integral-domain property of $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ is `std/integer/mod-n-polynomial.b4m`. The
> three hypotheses are the stated ones: $p$ is prime, $p$ misses a coefficient of each factor,
> and $p$ divides every coefficient of the product (which is what $p \mid d$ buys, given
> $d\,p(x) = c\,\alpha_1(x)\beta_1(x)$). The conclusion is the absurdity, so $d \neq 1$ is
> impossible.

> **One step of Judson's own is not his:** he writes $\alpha_1'(x)\beta_1'(x)$ where he means
> $(\alpha_1\beta_1)'(x)$, which is only legitimate because reduction commutes with
> multiplication. He leaves that to the reader; `ipoly.reduceIsMultiplicative` proves it, by
> induction on the convolution index — the two convolution recursions are the same recursion,
> and $\operatorname{cls}$ is an additive and multiplicative homomorphism termwise.

> **The $d = 1$ branch is PROVED.** Three earlier versions of this note named blockers that
> have all since been removed. The first said $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ are separate
> sorts with no embedding; that was **wrong about the design** — $\mathbb{Q}$ carries its
> integers as a *cut* (`isInteger`), so $d\,p(x) = c\,\alpha_1(x)\beta_1(x)$ is an ordinary
> $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ statement about a polynomial with integer coefficients. The second said it
> was blocked on a guard-relativization bug; that bug is **fixed**. The third said what
> remained was "polynomial plumbing, not a new idea" — which was right, and the plumbing is
> now built, in `std/rational/gauss.b4m`.
>
> `gaussLemmaAtDOne` concludes $p = a\,b$ with both factors monic of the stated degrees, on 40
> axioms with no holes. The two scalar cases differ in shape, so Judson's "the case in which
> $c = -1$ can be handled similarly" needed its own proof rather than a reuse:
>
> - $c = 1$ — the cofactors are the **same** unit ($a_m b_n = 1$). Either both are $1$, and
>   $\alpha_1, \beta_1$ serve as they stand, or both are $-1$, and their negations serve —
>   which is Judson's $p(x) = (-\alpha_1(x))(-\beta_1(x))$, now a theorem
>   (`theTwoNegationsCancelInAProduct`).
> - $c = -1$ — the cofactors are **opposite** units ($a_m b_n = -1$), so exactly ONE factor is
>   negated, not both.
>
> What $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ needed for this, none of which existed: the product's leading
> coefficient ($\operatorname{coeff}(pq, m{+}n) = \operatorname{coeff}(p,m)\operatorname{coeff}(q,n)$),
> and the negation algebra — negation pulls out of a convolution, hence out of a product, hence
> two negations cancel.
>
> **THE REDUCTION ITSELF IS NOW BUILT** (`std/rational/gauss-reduction.b4m`), so both of
> Judson's branches are reachable from one place rather than only stated separately.
>
> Applying Lemma 17.12 to *both* factors is two **models** over the one theorem. 17.12 is
> stated about `theSubject`, an uninterpreted nullary `func` with no defining axiom — a model
> *parameter* — so each model pins it to its own factor. The cost is exactly one discharge per
> model: of 17.12's 54 axioms only `denominatorMeaning` mentions the subject, so each model
> re-instances `isADenominatorFor` and discharges that one fact, and `--axioms` confirms the
> transferred theorems rest on 54 axioms each with just that one symbol swapped.
>
> Combining the scalars — $\frac{c_1}{d_1}\alpha_1 \cdot \frac{c_2}{d_2}\beta_1 =
> \frac{c_1c_2}{d_1d_2}\alpha_1\beta_1$ — is `theScalingsCombine`, resting on
> `theProductOfConstantMonomials`. $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ has associativity and commutativity but no
> multiplicative left-swap lemma, which is `assoc_commut`'s third required argument, so the
> regrouping is written as an explicit five-rewrite chain.
>
> **A real interface gap had to be closed to reach the $d \neq 1$ branch.** Lemma 17.12 reports
> `hasIntegerCoefficientsUpTo(q, succ(n))` — *bounded*, since its descent runs over a finite
> index range — while `theModulusCannotDivideEveryProductCoefficient` consumes the *unbounded*
> `hasIntegerCoefficients`, and the decomposition carries no degree bound on $q$ of its own.
> Bounded $\to$ unbounded is not sound in general. The bound is instead recovered from the
> scaling equation (`aNonzeroScalingReflectsTheDegreeBound`: above the bound the scaled
> coefficient vanishes, and `noZeroDivisors` with a nonzero scalar forces $q$'s coefficient to
> vanish too), after which the two integralities coincide, because above the bound every
> coefficient is $0$ and $0$ is an integer.

> **One shortfall, stated plainly.** The conclusion says "degree at most $m$, and the
> coefficient at $m$ is $1$" rather than using the `qIsMonic` predicate, because `qIsMonic`
> reads at $\operatorname{qdegree}(p)$ and the construction carries a *bound*, not an exact
> degree. For a polynomial whose degree IS $m$ the two say the same thing; converting between
> them needs the exact-degree bridge ($\deg_{\le}(p, m)$ and $\operatorname{coeff}(p,m) \neq 0$
> give $\deg p = m$), which exists at no layer of the corpus. If it is wanted it belongs in
> `std/ring/polynomial/leading.b4m` with the rest of that algebra.
>
> **How the model system charged for this.** $\mathbb{Q}[x]$'s leading-coefficient law could
> not be transferred from $F[x]$, because $F[x]$'s own version is a transfer through
> `FieldPolyModelsRingPoly` and 2b4m re-checks an inner transfer rather than composing models —
> the re-check then demands $\mathbb{Q}$ be mapped onto `ring.Ring` as a nested step, and fails
> (`std/ring.b4m:143: expected sort 'Field', got 'Q'`). Going one layer **down** fixes it: map
> $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ onto `ring_poly` directly, so there is one transfer and not two. The cost is
> that the re-check walks the source proof, so $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ must declare every helper
> `ring_poly` inducts on — `qtailConvolution` and `qsegment`, with their clauses, which
> $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ has no other use for.

> The reduction's own two theorems, re-exported so the chapter exercises them. The first fuses
> the two scalars Lemma 17.12 produces; the second is Judson's second paragraph, the

```2b4m
import qreduction <<< "std/rational/gauss-reduction.b4m"

// The two decompositions fuse into a single scaling: p = (c/d)·α1β1.
theorem theProductIsASingleScaling: forall a1, a2: qpoly.QPoly;
  forall r1, s1, r2, s2: qpoly.Q; forall n1, n2: peano.Nat;
  rationals.nonzero(s1) -> rationals.nonzero(s2) ->
  qpoly.isTheIntegerDecomposition(qreduction.THE_LEFT_FACTOR, a1, r1, s1, n1) ->
  qpoly.isTheIntegerDecomposition(qreduction.THE_RIGHT_FACTOR, a2, r2, s2, n2) ->
  qpoly.qmulP(qreduction.THE_LEFT_FACTOR, qreduction.THE_RIGHT_FACTOR) =
```

**Corollary 17.14 (Rational Root Theorem)** Let
$p(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ with $a_n \neq 0$. If $r/s$ is a zero of
$p(x)$ with $\gcd(r, s) = 1$, then $r \mid a_0$ and $s \mid a_n$.

> **PROVED.** Both halves: `theNumeratorDividesTheConstantTerm` and
> `theDenominatorDividesTheLeadingCoefficient`. The corollary does not use Gauss's Lemma at
> all — three earlier versions of this note said it waited on Gauss, and that was simply
> wrong; its proof is direct.
>
> "Multiply $p(r/s) = 0$ through by $s^{n+1}$" is `qclearedSum`, whose own recursion
> $S(k{+}1) = s\,(S(k) + a_k r^k)$ avoids index subtraction entirely. Then the two halves read
> that sum from opposite ends, and each finishes the same way — Bézout turns the *quantified*
> coprimality into a certificate, and a coprime-power induction strips every factor:
>
> - $r \mid a_0$: the constant-term split gives $0 = s^{n+1} a_0 + r t$, so $r \mid s^{n+1} a_0$,
>   and stripping every $s$ leaves $r \mid a_0$.
> - $s \mid a_n$: the *defining clause* gives $s\,(S(n) + a_n r^n) = 0$, so with $s \neq 0$ the
>   cofactor vanishes and $a_n r^n = -S(n)$ — which $s$ divides, since $s$ divides every
>   cleared sum. Stripping every $r$ leaves $s \mid a_n$.
>
> I had the wrong shape for the second half at first, reaching for a "leading-term split"
> symmetric to the first. But every term of $S(n{+}1)$ carries an $s$, so that split yields
> only $s \mid 0$: the $s^{n+1}$ normalization is right for $r \mid a_0$ and wrong for
> $s \mid a_n$, where the textbook proof scales by $s^n$. The defining clause supplies what is
> needed with no second recursion at all.
>
> **What this needed below it, none of which existed.** Neither $\mathbb{Z}$'s division
> algorithm nor its Bézout can transfer onto the cut — each reaches `nonnegCases`, itself a
> transfer of `peano.zeroOrSucc`, so the composed chain
> $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$'s NonNeg $\to \mathbb{Q}$'s isInteger wants `Nat` to be both
> `Int` and `Q` at once (`std/peano.b4m:857: expected sort 'Int', got 'Q'`). 2b4m re-checks an
> inner transfer rather than composing models, so this is structural. Both are therefore proved
> natively in `std/rational/integer-division.b4m` on `naturalWellOrdering` — the division
> algorithm by well-ordering the *reachable remainders*, Bézout by well-ordering the *positive
> combinations*. Euclid's Lemma itself (`euclidFromBezoutOnTheCut`) takes the certificate as a
> hypothesis, which is what keeps the whole development off `gcd`/`div`/`mod`.

**Theorem 17.15 (Eisenstein's Criterion)** Let $p$ be a prime and suppose that
$$f(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x].$$
If $p \mid a_i$ for $i = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$, but $p \nmid a_n$ and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then
$f(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

> **PROVED, in the form Gauss's reduction consumes.** Past versions of this note said the
> proof "needs $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ alongside $\mathbb{Z}[x]$"; that was **wrong** about Judson's
> argument, which never reduces mod $p$ — it works entirely in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ with
> divisibility of integer coefficients.
>
> The content is the minimal-index argument, and it turned out to need no new mathematics:
> `theDenominatorCannotExceedOne`, already proved for Gauss's $d \neq 1$ case, says a prime
> missing a coefficient of each factor cannot divide *every* coefficient of the product. So
> suppose $f = gh$ with both factors' degrees summing below $n$. Then every coefficient of $f$
> would be divisible by $p$ — below $n$ by hypothesis, and above the degree sum because the
> coefficient vanishes there and $p \mid 0$. That contradicts the theorem, so the degree sum
> cannot stay below $n$.
>
> **Stated positively, not as an absurdity.** `eisensteinForcesTheFullDegree` concludes
> "the degree bound cannot stay below $n$". My first draft concluded $1 \neq 1$ instead, which
> is a fake conclusion dressed as a contradiction — the positive form is what Gauss's
> reduction actually consumes, and it is honest about what was shown.
>
> What remains unformalized is the *packaging*: turning "no low-degree factorization in
> $\mathbb{Z}[x]$" into "irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$" is Gauss's Lemma applied once, and
> Gauss is proved (`std/rational/gauss.b4m`) — but the two speak different vocabularies
> ($\mathbb{Z}[x]$'s `IntPoly` against $\mathbb{Q}[x]$'s `QPoly` with an integrality cut), and
> bridging them is the embedding bookkeeping that §17.1's five-polynomial-layer duplication
> makes expensive. That is queue item 5's territory, not new mathematics.

## Ideals in $F[x]$

**Theorem 17.16** If $F$ is a field, then every ideal in $F[x]$ is a principal ideal.

**Proof.** Let $I$ be an ideal of $F[x]$. If $I$ is the zero ideal, the theorem is easily
true. Suppose that $I$ is a nontrivial ideal in $F[x]$, and let $p(x) \in I$ be a nonzero
element of minimal degree. If $\deg p(x) = 0$, then $p(x)$ is a nonzero constant and $1$ must
be in $I$. Since $1$ generates all of $F[x]$, $\langle 1 \rangle = I = F[x]$ and $I$ is again
a principal ideal.

Now assume that $\deg p(x) \geq 1$ and let $f(x)$ be any element in $I$. By the division
algorithm there exist $q(x)$ and $r(x)$ in $F[x]$ such that $f(x) = p(x) q(x) + r(x)$ and
$\deg r(x) < \deg p(x)$. Since $f(x), p(x) \in I$ and $I$ is an ideal,
$r(x) = f(x) - p(x) q(x)$ is also in $I$. However, since we chose $p(x)$ to be of minimal
degree, $r(x)$ must be the zero polynomial. Since we can write any element $f(x)$ in $I$ as
$p(x) q(x)$ for some $q(x) \in F[x]$, it must be the case that
$I = \langle p(x) \rangle$. $\blacksquare$

The ideal is carried as a membership predicate, the idiom §16.3 uses — there is no sort of
subsets of $F[x]$. His "let $p(x) \in I$ be a nonzero element of minimal degree" is still taken
as a hypothesis below.

> **The reason given here used to be wrong.** It said picking a least element of an ideal
> "needs well-ordering over a SET of polynomials, which is the comprehension limit §16.4 also
> meets". That mistakes what gets well-ordered: not the ideal, but the **degrees** of its
> nonzero members, which are naturals. `peano_order.wellOrdering` is a schema over a predicate
> on `Nat`, and a membership predicate is exactly what it can close over — so no comprehension
> is involved. §17.2's `gcd.aMinimalDegreeCombinationExists` proves the same shape of claim for
> linear combinations, on 7 axioms, hole-free.
>
> The minimal-degree ideal member is therefore **provable by the same two steps** (some natural
> is the degree of a nonzero member; well-order those degrees). It is left as a hypothesis here
> only because nothing below needs more than the hypothesis, not because the construction is
> out of reach — and `minimalityForcesTheRemainderToVanish` below already consumes it in
> exactly the form the construction would produce.

```2b4m
                  @given-r-is-lower |
                    assume less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) {
                      @minimality |
                        forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
                          (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))
                        [by hypothesis given-minimality]
                      @r-in-the-ideal |
                        inIdeal(r)
                        [by hypothesis given-r-in-the-ideal]
                      @r-is-lower |
                        less_than(degree(r), degree(p))
                        [by hypothesis given-r-is-lower]
                      // were r nonzero, minimality would forbid its lower degree
                      @minimality-at-r |
                        inIdeal(r) -> (not (r = ZEROP)) ->
                          (not less_than(degree(r), degree(p)))
                        [using specialize minimality(r)]
                      @given-r-nonzero |
                        assume not (r = ZEROP) {
                          @r-nonzero |
                            not (r = ZEROP)
                            [by hypothesis given-r-nonzero]
                          @r-is-not-lower |
                            not less_than(degree(r), degree(p))
                            [using tautology minimality-at-r r-in-the-ideal r-nonzero]
                          @contradiction |
                            r = ZEROP
                            [by ex_falso r-is-lower r-is-not-lower]
                        }
                      @nonzero-is-absurd |
                        (not (r = ZEROP)) -> r = ZEROP
                        [by discharge given-r-nonzero]
                      @conclusion-the-remainder-vanishes |
                        r = ZEROP
                        [using tautology nonzero-is-absurd]
                    }
                  @lower-implies-zero |
                    less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> r = ZEROP
                    [by discharge given-r-is-lower]
                }
              @in-the-ideal-implies |
                inIdeal(r) -> less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> r = ZEROP
                [by discharge given-r-in-the-ideal]
            }
          @minimality-implies |
            (forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
              (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))) ->
              inIdeal(r) -> less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> r = ZEROP
            [by discharge given-minimality]
        }
      @discharge-r |
        forall r9: Poly;
          (forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
          (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))) ->
          inIdeal(r9) -> less_than(degree(r9), degree(p)) -> r9 = ZEROP
        [by generalize generalize-r]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall p, r: Poly;
      (forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
      (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))) ->
      inIdeal(r) -> less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> r = ZEROP
    [by generalize generalize-p]
qed
```

"Since we can write any element $f(x)$ in $I$ as $p(x) q(x)$": given the division and the
minimality hypothesis, every member of the ideal is a multiple of $p$.

```2b4m
theorem everyIdealMemberIsAMultiple: forall f, p, q, r: Poly;
  (forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
  (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))) ->
  inIdeal(f) -> inIdeal(p) ->
  f = add(mul(p, q), r) ->
  less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) ->
  f = mul(p, q)
proof
  @generalize-f |
    fix f: Poly {
      @generalize-p |
        fix p: Poly {
          @generalize-q |
            fix q: Poly {
              @generalize-r |
                fix r: Poly {
                  @given-minimality |
                    assume forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
                      (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p))) {
                      @given-f-in |
                        assume inIdeal(f) {
                          @given-p-in |
                            assume inIdeal(p) {
                              @given-the-division |
                                assume f = add(mul(p, q), r) {
                                  @given-r-is-lower |
                                    assume less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) {
                                      @minimality |
                                        forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
                                          (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))
                                        [by hypothesis given-minimality]
                                      @f-in |
                                        inIdeal(f)
                                        [by hypothesis given-f-in]
                                      @p-in |
                                        inIdeal(p)
                                        [by hypothesis given-p-in]

                                        f = add(mul(p, q), r)
                                        [by hypothesis given-the-division]

                                        [by hypothesis given-r-is-lower]

                                        add(f, fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q))) =
                                          add(f, fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q)))
                                        [by reflexivity]

                                      // r = f - pq lies in the ideal...
                                      @the-difference-is-in-the-ideal |
                                        inIdeal(add(f, fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q))))
                                        [using specialize theRemainderLiesInTheIdeal(f, p, q) f-in p-in]
                                      // ...and that difference IS r, by the division
                                      // f - pq = r: substitute f = pq + r and cancel

                                        add(f, fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q))) =
                                          add(add(mul(p, q), r), fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q)))
                                        [by rewrite the-division reflexivity-of-the-difference]
                                      @the-cancellation |
                                        add(add(mul(p, q), r), fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q))) = r
                                        [using specialize theAddedTermCancels(mul(p, q), r)]
                                      @the-difference-is-r |
                                        add(f, fieldpoly.negP(mul(p, q))) = r
                                        [using chain the-difference-expanded the-cancellation]
                                      @r-is-in-the-ideal |
                                        inIdeal(r)
                                        [by rewrite the-difference-is-r the-difference-is-in-the-ideal]
                                      // so minimality forces it to vanish
                                      @r-vanishes |
                                        r = ZEROP
                                        [using specialize minimalityForcesTheRemainderToVanish(p, r) minimality r-is-in-the-ideal r-is-lower]
                                      @f-is-the-multiple-plus-zero |
                                        f = add(mul(p, q), ZEROP)
                                        [by rewrite r-vanishes the-division]
                                      @adding-zero |
                                        add(mul(p, q), ZEROP) = mul(p, q)
                                        [using specialize fieldpoly.polyAddZeroRight(mul(p, q))]
                                      @conclusion-f-is-a-multiple |
                                        f = mul(p, q)
                                        [using chain f-is-the-multiple-plus-zero adding-zero]
                                    }
                                  @lower-degree-implies-the-multiple |
                                    less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> f = mul(p, q)
                                    [by discharge given-r-is-lower]
                                }
                              @the-division-implies-the-multiple |
                                f = add(mul(p, q), r) ->
                                  less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> f = mul(p, q)
                                [by discharge given-the-division]
                            }
                          @p-in-the-ideal-implies-the-multiple |
                            inIdeal(p) -> f = add(mul(p, q), r) ->
                              less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> f = mul(p, q)
                            [by discharge given-p-in]

                    }
                  @minimality-implies-every-member-is-a-multiple |
                    (forall x: Poly; inIdeal(x) -> (not (x = ZEROP)) ->
                      (not less_than(degree(x), degree(p)))) ->
                      inIdeal(f) -> inIdeal(p) -> f = add(mul(p, q), r) ->
                      less_than(degree(r), degree(p)) -> f = mul(p, q)
                    [by discharge given-minimality]
                }

                  inIdeal(f) -> inIdeal(p) -> f = add(mul(p, q), r9) ->
```

**Theorem 17.17** Let $F$ be a field and suppose that $p(x) \in F[x]$. Then the ideal
generated by $p(x)$ is maximal if and only if $p(x)$ is irreducible.

**Proof.** Suppose that $p(x)$ generates a maximal ideal of $F[x]$. Then
$\langle p(x) \rangle$ is also a prime ideal of $F[x]$. Since a maximal ideal must be
properly contained inside $F[x]$, $p(x)$ cannot be a constant polynomial. Let us assume that
$p(x)$ factors into two polynomials of lesser degree, say $p(x) = f(x) g(x)$. Since
$\langle p(x) \rangle$ is a prime ideal one of these factors, say $f(x)$, is in
$\langle p(x) \rangle$ and therefore be a multiple of $p(x)$. But this would imply that
$\langle p(x) \rangle \subset \langle f(x) \rangle$, which is impossible since
$\langle p(x) \rangle$ is maximal.

Conversely, suppose that $p(x)$ is irreducible over $F[x]$. Let $I$ be an ideal in $F[x]$
containing $\langle p(x) \rangle$. By Theorem 17.16, $I$ is a principal ideal; hence,
$I = \langle f(x) \rangle$ for some $f(x) \in F[x]$. Since $p(x) \in I$, it must be the case
that $p(x) = f(x) g(x)$ for some $g(x) \in F[x]$. However, $p(x)$ is irreducible; hence,
either $f(x)$ or $g(x)$ is a constant polynomial. If $f(x)$ is constant, then $I = F[x]$ and
we are done. If $g(x)$ is constant, then $f(x)$ is a constant multiple of $I$ and
$I = \langle p(x) \rangle$. Thus, there are no proper ideals of $F[x]$ that properly contain
$\langle p(x)\rangle$. $\blacksquare$

> **Ideals are membership predicates and primality is a hypothesis.** An ideal here is a
> `pred`, not a value (the §16.3 idiom), so "$I = \langle f \rangle$" reads as "every member
> of $I$ is a multiple of $f$" and "$I = F[x]$" as "$f$ divides every polynomial". His
> "$\langle p(x) \rangle$ is also a prime ideal" cites §16.4 rather than reproving it, and
> §16.4's route to that result runs through the factor ring $R/I$ over `ring.Ring` — a sort
> $F[x]$ is not, and one a model cannot reach, since `std/ring/factor.b4m` declares `Quot`
> and `cls` over `Ring` directly. So primality's *consequence* is what travels: a factor of
> strictly smaller degree is never a multiple of $p$, which is the impossibility his
> containment argument names.

```2b4m

  (not (p = ZEROP)) -> (not (h = ZEROP)) ->
  less_than(degree(f), degree(p)) ->
  (not (f = mul(p, h)))
proof
  @conclusion |
    forall p, f, h: Poly;
      (not (p = ZEROP)) -> (not (h = ZEROP)) ->

      (not (f = mul(p, h)))
```
