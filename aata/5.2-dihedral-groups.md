# Dihedral Groups

A literate `2b4m` transliteration of **Chapter 5 ("Permutation Groups"), §5.2
"Dihedral Groups"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract Algebra: Theory and
Applications* (source: `vendor/aata/src/permute.xml`; © 1997–2026 Judson, GFDL
1.3). Run `2b4m check aata/5.2-dihedral-groups.md` to verify.

Another special type of permutation group is the dihedral group. Such groups
consist of the rigid motions of a regular $n$-sided polygon. For $n = 3, 4,
\ldots$, the **$n$th dihedral group** $D_n$ is the group of rigid motions of a
regular $n$-gon.

Number the vertices of a regular $n$-gon $1, 2, \ldots, n$. There are exactly
$n$ choices to replace the first vertex; if the first vertex is replaced by $k$,
the second must be replaced by either $k+1$ or $k-1$. Hence there are $2n$
possible rigid motions.

> **Theorem.** The dihedral group $D_n$ is a subgroup of $S_n$ of order $2n$.

**The counting, encoded.** "Of order $2n$" is proved as a LISTING, the way §5.1
counts $S_n$ and $A_n$: the vertices are the letters $\{0, \ldots, n-1\}$ (numerals in
the segment), the rotation $\rho$ is the $n$-cycle $(0\ 1 \cdots n{-}1)$ on them, and a
**rigid motion** is a permutation of the $n$ letters that sends adjacent vertices
(one is $\rho$ of the other) to adjacent vertices. `std/permutation/dihedral.b4m`
proves, for $n \geq 3$, that some sequence of $n + n$ entries lists the rigid motions
— every entry is one, no entry repeats, every motion is an entry — by Judson's
argument exactly: the entries are the $n$ powers $\rho^k$ and the $n$ maps
$\rho^k \sigma$ with $\sigma$ the reflection fixing vertex $0$ ($i \mapsto n - i$);
a motion sends vertex $0$ to some $k$ and vertex $1$ to a neighbour $\rho(k)$ or
$\rho^{-1}(k)$ of it, and "everything else is forced" is an induction along the
letters (`walkingTheLetters`): $t(i+2)$ is a neighbour of $t(i+1) = u(i+1)$ other than
$t(i) = u(i)$, so it is $u(i+2)$. The subgroup half is the permutation group of §5.1
restricted to the motions; the order is the theorem below.

```2b4m
import permutation_dihedral <<< "std/permutation/dihedral.b4m"

// |D_n| = 2n, n ≥ 3: forall n; 2 < n -> exists rho, sigma; isTheRotation(rho, n) and
// isTheReflection(sigma, n) and exists w: Seq; (the first n + n entries of w are rigid
// motions of the n-gon) and (they are pairwise distinct) and (every rigid motion is one).
theorem dihedralGroupHasTwiceNElements = permutation_dihedral.dihedralGroupHasTwiceNElements
```

## The presentation

> **Theorem.** The group $D_n$, $n \geq 3$, consists of all products of the two
> elements $r$ and $s$, where $r$ has order $n$ and $s$ has order $2$, and these
> two elements satisfy the relation $srs = r^{-1}$.

Judson derives the relations from the geometry: $r$ is the rotation by
$360°/n$, $s$ the reflection fixing vertex 1, and a motion sending the first
vertex to $k$ sends the second to $k \pm 1$, so it is $r^k$ or $r^k s$.

That derivation is geometric and needs the vertex-counting above, so it is not
transcribed. What IS transcribed is everything the relations then give you —
which is the part of the theorem with mathematical content, and the part the rest
of the book uses. We take the relations as the presentation of $D_n$ (as a
presentation is, in fact, normally given) and prove from them.

```2b4m
import group <<< "std/group.b4m"
import group_power <<< "std/group/power.b4m"
import integer <<< "std/integer.b4m"

sort G = group.Grp
const e = group.E
func op = group.op
func inv = group.inverse
theorem invProduct = group.invProduct
theorem invInvolution = group.invInvolution
theorem opIdentityRight = group.opIdentityRight
theorem opInverseRight = group.opInverseRight
theorem inverseUnique = group.inverseUnique
axiom opAssoc = group.opAssoc
axiom opIdentityLeft = group.opIdentityLeft
axiom opInverseLeft = group.opInverseLeft

sort Int = group_power.Int
const ZERO = group_power.ZERO
func succ = group_power.succ
func add = group_power.add
func power = group_power.power
theorem powerAdd = group_power.powerAdd

// the two generators and the DEFINING RELATIONS
const r: G
const s: G
axiom sIsAnInvolution: op(s, s) = e
axiom reflectionInverts: op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)

// Judson leaves this as an exercise: srs = r^{-1} means s r^{-1} s = r too,
// i.e. conjugation by s inverts every power of r. Start with the base shape.
// s is its own inverse (`s^2 = 1`), which the relation arguments use repeatedly.
theorem sIsItsOwnInverse: inv(s) = s
proof
  @s-squared |
    op(s, s) = e
    [by cite sIsAnInvolution]
  @s-is-the-inverse |
    s = inv(s)
    [using specialize inverseUnique(s, s) s-squared]
  @conclusion |
    inv(s) = s
    [by symmetry s-is-the-inverse]
qed

// Judson leaves `srs = r^{-1}` as an exercise and uses it freely. Its mirror —
// conjugating r^{-1} by s gives r — follows by inverting both sides, since
// (s r s)^{-1} = s^{-1} r^{-1} s^{-1} = s r^{-1} s when s is an involution.
theorem sInvertsRInverse: op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = r
proof
  @s-inverts-r |
    op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)
    [by cite reflectionInverts]
  // invert both sides of srs = r^-1
  @inverse-of-r-inverse |
    inv(inv(r)) = r
    [using specialize invInvolution(r)]
  // and expand the left: (s r s)^-1 = s^-1 (s r)^-1 = s^-1 r^-1 s^-1
  @outer |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(inv(s), inv(op(s, r)))
    [using specialize invProduct(op(s, r), s)]
  @inner |
    inv(op(s, r)) = op(inv(r), inv(s))
    [using specialize invProduct(s, r)]
  @expanded |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(inv(s), op(inv(r), inv(s)))
    [by rewrite inner outer]
  @s-is-its-own-inverse |
    inv(s) = s
    [by cite sIsItsOwnInverse]
  @expanded-with-s |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(s, op(inv(r), s))
    [by rewrite s-is-its-own-inverse expanded]
  @assoc |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = op(s, op(inv(r), s))
    [using specialize opAssoc(s, inv(r), s)]
  @regroup |
    op(s, op(inv(r), s)) = op(op(s, inv(r)), s)
    [by symmetry assoc]
  // (s r s)^-1 = s r^-1 s ...
  @lhs-is-the-target |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(op(s, inv(r)), s)
    [using chain expanded-with-s regroup]
  // ... and (s r s)^-1 = (r^-1)^-1 = r
  @lhs-is-r |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = r
    [by rewrite s-inverts-r inverse-of-r-inverse]
  @flipped |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = inv(op(op(s, r), s))
    [by symmetry lhs-is-the-target]
  @conclusion |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = r
    [using chain flipped lhs-is-r]
qed

// Judson: "D_n consists of all finite products of r and s". The content of that
// claim, in a group where only the relations hold, is CLOSURE: the two shapes
// r^k and r^k·s absorb multiplication by either generator. Multiplying by r on
// the right is immediate for the first shape.
theorem rotationTimesRotation: forall j: Int; forall k: Int;
  op(power(r, j), power(r, k)) = power(r, add(j, k))
proof
  @the-law |
    forall g: G; forall m: Int; forall n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
    [by cite powerAdd]
  @conclusion |
    forall j: Int; forall k: Int; op(power(r, j), power(r, k)) = power(r, add(j, k))
    [by apply_at(r) the-law]
qed

// The relation in the form the closure argument actually uses: moving an s PAST
// an r inverts the r. (`srs = r^-1` with the trailing s cancelled.)
theorem sPastR: op(s, r) = op(inv(r), s)
proof
  @relation |
    op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)
    [by cite reflectionInverts]
  // ((s r) s) s = (s r) (s s) = (s r) e = s r
  @reflexivity-of-the-product |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(op(s, r), s), s)
    [by reflexivity]
  @regroup |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(s, r), op(s, s))
    [using specialize opAssoc(op(s, r), s, s)]
  @s-squared |
    op(s, s) = e
    [by cite sIsAnInvolution]
  @with-identity |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(s, r), e)
    [by rewrite s-squared regroup]
  @identity-on-the-right |
    op(op(s, r), e) = op(s, r)
    [using specialize opIdentityRight(op(s, r))]
  @left-side-is-s-r |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(s, r)
    [using chain with-identity identity-on-the-right]
  // the SAME expression, read the other way: ((s r) s)·s = (r^-1)·s by the relation
  @s-r-s-times-s |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(inv(r), s)
    [by rewrite relation reflexivity-of-the-product]
  @flipped |
    op(s, r) = op(op(op(s, r), s), s)
    [by symmetry left-side-is-s-r]
  @conclusion |
    op(s, r) = op(inv(r), s)
    [using chain flipped s-r-s-times-s]
qed
```

## What the relations give, and what they do not

The four results above are the whole of §5.2 that survives without finite
cardinality:

- `sIsItsOwnInverse` — from $s^2 = 1$, which Judson states and uses freely;
- `sInvertsRInverse` — conjugating $r^{-1}$ by $s$ gives $r$. Judson leaves the
  relation itself as an exercise and never states this mirror, but every later
  manipulation needs it;
- `rotationTimesRotation` — the rotations are closed, by the laws of exponents;
- `sPastR` — **the load-bearing one.** Moving a reflection past a rotation
  inverts the rotation. This is what makes Judson's two shapes $r^k$ and $r^k s$
  closed under multiplication, which is the content of "consists of all products
  of $r$ and $s$".

What the relations alone do not give — and the presentation cannot, since they
describe every dihedral group at once, which is precisely why a presentation is
stated with $r^n = 1$ as a third relation — is done GEOMETRICALLY in
`std/permutation/dihedral.b4m` on the concrete $n$-gon: $|D_n| = 2n$, that the $n$
rotations and $n$ reflections EXHAUST the group, and that $r$ has order exactly $n$
(the powers $\rho^0, \ldots, \rho^{n-1}$ are pairwise distinct: they send vertex $0$
to different vertices). The concrete $\sigma$ and $\rho$ satisfy the relations —
`reflectionSquaredIsIdentity` is $s^2 = 1$ and `reflectionReversesTheRotation`
is $\sigma\rho = \rho^{-1}\sigma$ on the vertices, the pointwise form of $srs = r^{-1}$.
Still prose: the even/odd vertex case split on which reflections pass through
vertices and which through edges.
