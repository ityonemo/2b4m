# Dihedral Groups

A literate `2b4m` transliteration of **Chapter 5 ("Permutation Groups"), §5.2
"Dihedral Groups"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract Algebra: Theory and
Applications* (source: `vendor/aata/src/permute.xml`; © 1997–2026 Judson, GFDL
1.3). Run `2b4m check aata/5.2-dihedral-groups.md` to verify.

Another special type of permutation group is the dihedral group. Recall the symmetry group
of an equilateral triangle in Chapter 3. Such groups consist of the rigid motions of a
regular $n$-sided polygon. For $n = 3, 4,
\ldots$, the **$n$th dihedral group** $D_n$ is the group of rigid motions of a
regular $n$-gon.

Number the vertices of a regular $n$-gon $1, 2, \ldots, n$. There are exactly
$n$ choices to replace the first vertex; if the first vertex is replaced by $k$,
the second must be replaced by either $k+1$ or $k-1$. Hence there are $2n$
possible rigid motions.

> **Theorem.**
> 1. The dihedral group, $D_n$, is a subgroup of $S_n$ of order $2n$.
> 2. The group $D_n$, $n \geq 3$, consists of all products of the two elements $r$ and $s$,
>    where $r$ has order $n$ and $s$ has order $2$, and these two elements satisfy the
>    relation $srs = r^{-1}$.

**Proof.** The possible motions of a regular $n$-gon are either reflections or rotations.
There are exactly $n$ possible rotations:
$$\identity, \frac{360^{\circ}}{n}, 2 \cdot \frac{360^{\circ}}{n}, \ldots, (n-1) \cdot \frac{360^{\circ}}{n}.$$
We will denote the rotation $360^{\circ}/n$ by $r$. The rotation $r$ generates all of the
other rotations. That is,
$$r^k = k \cdot \frac{360^{\circ}}{n}.$$
Label the $n$ reflections $s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n$, where $s_k$ is the reflection that leaves
vertex $k$ fixed. There are two cases of reflections, depending on whether $n$ is even or
odd. If there are an even number of vertices, then two vertices are left fixed by a
reflection, and $s_1 = s_{n/2 + 1}, s_2 = s_{n/2 + 2}, \ldots, s_{n/2} = s_n$. If there are
an odd number of vertices, then only a single vertex is left fixed by a reflection and
$s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n$ are distinct. In either case, the order of each $s_k$ is two. Let
$s = s_1$. Then $s^2 = 1$ and $r^n = 1$. Since any rigid motion $t$ of the $n$-gon replaces
the first vertex by the vertex $k$, the second vertex must be replaced by either $k+1$ or by
$k-1$. If the second vertex is replaced by $k+1$, then $t = r^k$. If the second vertex is
replaced by $k-1$, then $t = r^k s$. Hence, $r$ and $s$ generate $D_n$. That is, $D_n$
consists of all finite products of $r$ and $s$,
$$D_n = \{1, r, r^2, \ldots, r^{n-1}, s, rs, r^2 s, \ldots, r^{n-1} s\}.$$
We will leave the proof that $srs = r^{-1}$ as an exercise. $\blacksquare$

> *Judson numbers the vertices $1, \ldots, n$; here they are the letters
> $0, \ldots, n-1$. And there being no cardinality function, "of order $2n$" is
> stated as a listing of $n + n$ rigid motions, no entry repeated, in which every
> rigid motion appears.*
>
> *His proof derives the relations from the geometry of the $n$-gon. We are instead taking
> them as the PRESENTATION of $D_n$ and deriving everything from them — a divergent
> axiomatic strategy, since the geometry is not available to us. The formal content below
> establishes the listing by induction along the vertices: a motion sends vertex $0$ to some
> $k$ and vertex $1$ to a neighbour of it, and everything else is then forced.*

```2b4m
import permutation_dihedral <<< "std/permutation/dihedral.b4m"

// |D_n| = 2n, n ≥ 3: forall n; 2 < n -> exists rho, sigma; isTheRotation(rho, n) and
// isTheReflection(sigma, n) and exists w: Seq; (the first n + n entries of w are rigid
// motions of the n-gon) and (they are pairwise distinct) and (every rigid motion is one).
fact dihedralGroupHasTwiceNElements = permutation_dihedral.dihedralGroupHasTwiceNElements
```

## The presentation

> *Part (2) of the Theorem above is what the rest of this section rests on: $D_n$ is generated
> by $r$ and $s$ subject to $r^n = 1$, $s^2 = 1$ and $srs = r^{-1}$.*

> *Taking those relations as the PRESENTATION of $D_n$, rather than deriving them from the
> $n$-gon as Judson does, is the divergence declared above. Everything below follows from
> the relations alone.*

```2b4m
import group <<< "std/group.b4m"
import group_power <<< "std/group/power.b4m"

sort G = group.Grp
const e = group.E
func op = group.op
func inv = group.inverse
fact invProduct = group.invProduct
fact invInvolution = group.invInvolution
fact opIdentityRight = group.opIdentityRight
fact opInverseRight = group.opInverseRight
fact inverseUnique = group.inverseUnique
fact opAssoc = group.opAssoc
fact opIdentityLeft = group.opIdentityLeft
fact opInverseLeft = group.opInverseLeft

sort Int = group_power.Int
const ZERO = group_power.ZERO
func succ = group_power.succ
func add = group_power.add
func power = group_power.power
fact powerAdd = group_power.powerAdd

// the two generators and the DEFINING RELATIONS
const r: G
const s: G
axiom sIsAnInvolution: op(s, s) = e
axiom reflectionInverts: op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)

// Judson leaves this as an exercise: srs = r^{-1} means s r^{-1} s = r too,
// i.e. conjugation by s inverts every power of r. Start with the base shape.
// s is its own inverse (`s^2 = 1`), which the relation arguments use repeatedly.
theorem sIsItsOwnInverse: inv(s) = s
proof
  @s-squared |
    op(s, s) = e
    [by cite sIsAnInvolution]
  @s-is-the-inverse |
    s = inv(s)
    [using specialize inverseUnique(s, s) s-squared]
  @conclusion |
    inv(s) = s
    [by symmetry s-is-the-inverse]
qed

// Judson leaves `srs = r^{-1}` as an exercise and uses it freely. Its mirror —
// conjugating r^{-1} by s gives r — follows by inverting both sides, since
// (s r s)^{-1} = s^{-1} r^{-1} s^{-1} = s r^{-1} s when s is an involution.
theorem sInvertsRInverse: op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = r
proof
  @s-inverts-r |
    op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)
    [by cite reflectionInverts]
  // invert both sides of srs = r^-1
  @inverse-of-r-inverse |
    inv(inv(r)) = r
    [using specialize invInvolution(r)]
  // and expand the left: (s r s)^-1 = s^-1 (s r)^-1 = s^-1 r^-1 s^-1
  @outer |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(inv(s), inv(op(s, r)))
    [using specialize invProduct(op(s, r), s)]
  @inner |
    inv(op(s, r)) = op(inv(r), inv(s))
    [using specialize invProduct(s, r)]
  @expanded |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(inv(s), op(inv(r), inv(s)))
    [by rewrite inner outer]
  @s-is-its-own-inverse |
    inv(s) = s
    [by cite sIsItsOwnInverse]
  @expanded-with-s |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(s, op(inv(r), s))
    [by rewrite s-is-its-own-inverse expanded]
  @assoc |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = op(s, op(inv(r), s))
    [using specialize opAssoc(s, inv(r), s)]
  @regroup |
    op(s, op(inv(r), s)) = op(op(s, inv(r)), s)
    [by symmetry assoc]
  // (s r s)^-1 = s r^-1 s ...
  @lhs-is-the-target |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = op(op(s, inv(r)), s)
    [using chain expanded-with-s regroup]
  // ... and (s r s)^-1 = (r^-1)^-1 = r
  @lhs-is-r |
    inv(op(op(s, r), s)) = r
    [by rewrite s-inverts-r inverse-of-r-inverse]
  @flipped |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = inv(op(op(s, r), s))
    [by symmetry lhs-is-the-target]
  @conclusion |
    op(op(s, inv(r)), s) = r
    [using chain flipped lhs-is-r]
qed

// Judson: "D_n consists of all finite products of r and s". The content of that
// claim, in a group where only the relations hold, is CLOSURE: the two shapes
// r^k and r^k·s absorb multiplication by either generator. Multiplying by r on
// the right is immediate for the first shape.
theorem rotationTimesRotation: forall j: Int; forall k: Int;
  op(power(r, j), power(r, k)) = power(r, add(j, k))
proof
  @the-law |
    forall g: G; forall m: Int; forall n: Int; op(power(g, m), power(g, n)) = power(g, add(m, n))
    [by cite powerAdd]
  @conclusion |
    forall j: Int; forall k: Int; op(power(r, j), power(r, k)) = power(r, add(j, k))
    [by apply_at(r) the-law]
qed

// The relation in the form the closure argument actually uses: moving an s PAST
// an r inverts the r. (`srs = r^-1` with the trailing s cancelled.)
theorem sPastR: op(s, r) = op(inv(r), s)
proof
  @relation |
    op(op(s, r), s) = inv(r)
    [by cite reflectionInverts]
  // ((s r) s) s = (s r) (s s) = (s r) e = s r
  @reflexivity-of-the-product |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(op(s, r), s), s)
    [by reflexivity]
  @regroup |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(s, r), op(s, s))
    [using specialize opAssoc(op(s, r), s, s)]
  @s-squared |
    op(s, s) = e
    [by cite sIsAnInvolution]
  @with-identity |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(op(s, r), e)
    [by rewrite s-squared regroup]
  @identity-on-the-right |
    op(op(s, r), e) = op(s, r)
    [using specialize opIdentityRight(op(s, r))]
  @left-side-is-s-r |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(s, r)
    [using chain with-identity identity-on-the-right]
  // the SAME expression, read the other way: ((s r) s)·s = (r^-1)·s by the relation
  @s-r-s-times-s |
    op(op(op(s, r), s), s) = op(inv(r), s)
    [by rewrite relation reflexivity-of-the-product]
  @flipped |
    op(s, r) = op(op(op(s, r), s), s)
    [by symmetry left-side-is-s-r]
  @conclusion |
    op(s, r) = op(inv(r), s)
    [using chain flipped s-r-s-times-s]
qed
```

## The relation on the $n$-gon

Judson leaves the relation $srs = r^{-1}$ as an exercise. On the concrete $n$-gon it is
a short computation: reflecting, rotating, and reflecting back reverses the rotation.

**Proposition.** *Let $\rho$ be the rotation and $\sigma$ the reflection of a regular
$n$-gon. Then $\sigma\rho\sigma = \rho^{-1}$.*

**Proof.** A reflection carries each rotation to its inverse when moved past it,
$\sigma\rho = \rho^{-1}\sigma$, and a reflection is its own inverse,
$\sigma\sigma = 1$. So $\sigma\rho\sigma = (\sigma\rho)\sigma =
(\rho^{-1}\sigma)\sigma = \rho^{-1}(\sigma\sigma) = \rho^{-1}$.

```2b4m
import permutation_dihedral_orders <<< "std/permutation/dihedral-orders.b4m"

sort Fn = permutation_dihedral_orders.Fn
func compose = permutation_dihedral_orders.compose
func funcInverse = permutation_dihedral_orders.inverse
const identityFn = permutation_dihedral_orders.identityFn
sort Nat = permutation_dihedral_orders.Nat
pred isTheRotation = permutation_dihedral_orders.isTheRotation
pred isTheReflection = permutation_dihedral_orders.isTheReflection
fact reflectionReversesRotationAsMaps = permutation_dihedral_orders.reflectionReversesRotationAsMaps
fact reflectionSquaredIsIdentity = permutation_dihedral_orders.reflectionSquaredIsIdentity
fact composeAssoc = permutation_dihedral_orders.composeAssoc
fact composeIdentityRight = permutation_dihedral_orders.composeIdentityRight

theorem reflectionConjugationInvertsTheRotation: forall rho: Fn; forall sigma: Fn; forall n: Nat;
  isTheRotation(rho, n) -> isTheReflection(sigma, n) ->
  compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
proof
  @generalize-the-rotation |
    fix rho: Fn {
      @generalize-the-reflection |
        fix sigma: Fn {
          @generalize-the-vertex-count |
            fix n: Nat {
              @given-rotation |
                assume isTheRotation(rho, n) {
                  @given-reflection |
                    assume isTheReflection(sigma, n) {
                      @rotation |
                        isTheRotation(rho, n)
                        [by hypothesis given-rotation]
                      @reflection |
                        isTheReflection(sigma, n)
                        [by hypothesis given-reflection]
                      @reflection-moves-past-the-rotation |
                        compose(sigma, rho) = compose(funcInverse(rho), sigma)
                        [using specialize reflectionReversesRotationAsMaps(rho, sigma, n) rotation reflection]
                      @reflecting-twice-is-the-identity |
                        compose(sigma, sigma) = identityFn
                        [using specialize reflectionSquaredIsIdentity(sigma, n) reflection]
                      @conjugate-regrouped-around-the-reflections |
                        compose(compose(funcInverse(rho), sigma), sigma) = compose(funcInverse(rho), compose(sigma, sigma))
                        [using specialize composeAssoc(funcInverse(rho), sigma, sigma)]
                      @conjugate-with-the-reflections-cancelled |
                        compose(compose(funcInverse(rho), sigma), sigma) = compose(funcInverse(rho), identityFn)
                        [by rewrite reflecting-twice-is-the-identity conjugate-regrouped-around-the-reflections]
                      @the-identity-drops-off |
                        compose(funcInverse(rho), identityFn) = funcInverse(rho)
                        [using specialize composeIdentityRight(funcInverse(rho))]
                      @conjugate-past-the-reflection-is-the-inverse-rotation |
                        compose(compose(funcInverse(rho), sigma), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
                        [using chain conjugate-with-the-reflections-cancelled the-identity-drops-off]
                      @conclusion |
                        compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
                        [by rewrite reflection-moves-past-the-rotation conjugate-past-the-reflection-is-the-inverse-rotation]
                    }
                  @discharge-the-reflection-hypothesis |
                    isTheReflection(sigma, n) -> compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
                    [by discharge given-reflection]
                }
              @discharge-the-rotation-hypothesis |
                isTheRotation(rho, n) -> isTheReflection(sigma, n) -> compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
                [by discharge given-rotation]
            }
          @discharge-the-vertex-count |
            forall n: Nat; isTheRotation(rho, n) -> isTheReflection(sigma, n) -> compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
            [by generalize generalize-the-vertex-count]
        }
      @discharge-the-reflection |
        forall sigma: Fn; forall n: Nat; isTheRotation(rho, n) -> isTheReflection(sigma, n) -> compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
        [by generalize generalize-the-reflection]
    }
  @conclusion |
    forall rho: Fn; forall sigma: Fn; forall n: Nat; isTheRotation(rho, n) -> isTheReflection(sigma, n) -> compose(compose(sigma, rho), sigma) = funcInverse(rho)
    [by generalize generalize-the-rotation]
qed
```

> *What the relations alone do not give — and the presentation cannot, since they describe
> every dihedral group at once, which is why a presentation is stated with $r^n = 1$ as a
> third relation — are the finite facts: $|D_n| = 2n$, that the $n$ rotations and $n$
> reflections exhaust the group, and that $r$ has order exactly $n$, the powers $\rho^0,
> \ldots, \rho^{n-1}$ being pairwise distinct since they send vertex $0$ to different
> vertices.*

## The Motion Group of a Cube

We can investigate the groups of rigid motions of geometric objects other than a regular
$n$-sided polygon to obtain interesting examples of permutation groups. Let us consider the
group of rigid motions of a cube. By rigid motion, we mean a rotation with the axis of
rotation about opposing faces, edges, or vertices. One of the first questions that we can
ask about this group is *what is its order?* A cube has $6$ sides. If a particular side is
facing upward, then there are four possible rotations of the cube that will preserve the
upward-facing side. Hence, the order of the group is $6 \cdot 4 = 24$. We have just proved
the following proposition.

> **Proposition.** The group of rigid motions of a cube contains $24$ elements.

> **Theorem.** The group of rigid motions of a cube is $S_4$.

**Proof.** From the previous proposition, we already know that the motion group of the cube
has $24$ elements, the same number of elements as there are in $S_4$. There are exactly
four diagonals in the cube. If we label these diagonals $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$, we must
show that the motion group of the cube will give us any permutation of the diagonals. If we
can obtain all of these permutations, then $S_4$ and the group of rigid motions of the cube
must be the same. To obtain a transposition we can rotate the cube $180^{\circ}$ about the
axis joining the midpoints of opposite edges. There are six such axes, giving all
transpositions in $S_4$. Since every element in $S_4$ is the product of a finite number of
transpositions, the motion group of a cube must be $S_4$. $\blacksquare$
