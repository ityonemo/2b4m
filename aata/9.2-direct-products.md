# Direct Products

A literate `2b4m` transliteration of **Chapter 9 ("Isomorphisms"), §9.2 "Direct
Products"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications*
(source: `vendor/aata/src/isomorph.xml`, lines 448–917; © 1997–2026 Judson, GFDL 1.3).
Run `2b4m check aata/9.2-direct-products.md` to verify.

The direct product lives in `std/group/product.b4m`.

## External direct products

**Definition.** Let $G$ and $H$ be groups. The **external direct product**
$G \times H$ is the set of ordered pairs $(g, h)$ with $g \in G$ and $h \in H$, under
the componentwise operation
$$(g_1, h_1)(g_2, h_2) = (g_1 g_2,\ h_1 h_2).$$

As in §9.1, the two factors are *declared* independently rather than aliased from one
import — identity-by-origin would otherwise collapse them to a single sort, and a
product of a group with itself is not what the theory is about. `product.b4m` declares
both and keeps them symmetric, which is the content.

```2b4m
import product <<< "std/group/product.b4m"

// the two factors and their structure
sort G = product.G
sort H = product.H
const eG = product.EG
const eH = product.EH
func opG = product.opG
func opH = product.opH

// the product: pairs, their projections, and the componentwise operation
sort Pair = product.Pair
func pair = product.pair
func fst = product.fst
func snd = product.snd
func prodOp = product.prodOp
const prodE = product.prodE
```

The componentwise operation really is componentwise — the projections of a product are
the products of the projections:

```2b4m
theorem fstOfProduct = product.fstOfProduct
theorem sndOfProduct = product.sndOfProduct
```

**Proposition 9.13.** $G \times H$ is a group under the componentwise operation.

The three group laws are proved over the pair sort, and `model ProductGroup` then makes
$G \times H$ a group so `std/group.b4m`'s whole corpus transfers to it:

```2b4m
theorem prodAssoc = product.prodAssoc
theorem prodIdentityLeft = product.prodIdentityLeft
theorem prodInverseLeft = product.prodInverseLeft
```

Judson notes the identity is $(e, e)$ and the inverse of $(g, h)$ is
$(g^{-1}, h^{-1})$; the right-handed laws and cancellation come through the model
rather than being re-proved:

```2b4m
theorem prodIdentityRight = product.prodIdentityRight
theorem prodInverseRight = product.prodInverseRight
theorem prodCancelLeft = product.prodCancelLeft
```

## Theorem 9.17 — the order of a tuple

**Theorem 9.17.** Let $(g_1, \ldots, g_n) \in \prod G_i$. If $g_i$ has finite order
$r_i$ in $G_i$, then the order of $(g_1, \ldots, g_n)$ in $\prod G_i$ is the least
common multiple of $r_1, \ldots, r_n$.

**Example.** Let $(8, 56) \in \mathbb{Z}_{12} \times \mathbb{Z}_{60}$. Since
$\gcd(8,12) = 4$, the order of 8 is $12/4 = 3$; similarly the order of 56 in
$\mathbb{Z}_{60}$ is 15. The least common multiple of 3 and 15 is 15, so $(8,56)$ has
order 15.

The theorem rests on a single structural fact: a pair is the identity exactly when
*both* components are. So the exponents that kill $(g, h)$ are precisely the common
multiples of the two component orders, and the least of those is the lcm.

```2b4m
theorem pairIsIdentityIffComponentsAre = product.pairIsIdentityIffComponentsAre
```

The step from there to "the order *is* the lcm" additionally needs `power` over the
pair sort and the lcm's minimality, which `std/integer/lattice.b4m` has for integers
but which has not been connected to a product's element orders. The $k = 3$ instance
is available as a worked case:

```2b4m
theorem cubeIsIdentityComponentwise = product.cubeIsIdentityComponentwise
```

## Theorem 9.21 — when a product of cyclic groups is cyclic

**Theorem 9.21.** The group $\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n$ is isomorphic to
$\mathbb{Z}_{mn}$ if and only if $\gcd(m,n) = 1$.

*Proof sketch (Judson).* For the forward direction he proves the contrapositive: if
$\gcd(m,n) = d > 1$ then $mn/d$ is divisible by both $m$ and $n$, so
$(a,b) + \cdots + (a,b)$ ($mn/d$ times) $= (0,0)$ for *every* $(a,b)$ — no element has
order $mn$, so the product is not cyclic.

Not transliterated. It needs an isomorphism *between two concrete groups*
($\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n$ and $\mathbb{Z}_{mn}$) with $m$ and $n$ as
parameters, and `std/integer/mod-n.b4m` fixes its modulus as a single constant `N` —
there is one $\mathbb{Z}_n$ per file, not a family indexed by $n$. `std/integer/mod-n-product.b4m`
models ℤ_n × ℤ_n for a single modulus, which is the diagonal case rather than the
general one. See `CHAPTER9-PAINPOINTS`.

**Example.** $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}_6$, because $(1,1)$
generates the product — unlike $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$, which is not
isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_4$. Both are concrete computations in small groups.

## Internal direct products

Judson's §9.2 closes with **internal** direct products: $G$ is the internal direct
product of subgroups $H$ and $K$ when $G = HK$, $H \cap K = \{e\}$, and $hk = kh$ for
all $h \in H$, $k \in K$ — and then $G \cong H \times K$. This needs subgroup
*products* and *intersections* as subgroups, which `std/subgroup.b4m` expresses as
membership predicates; the theorem is an isomorphism between a group and a product,
which runs into the same two-concrete-groups limit as Theorem 9.21. Recorded as prose.
