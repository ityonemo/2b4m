# The Method of Repeated Squares

A literate transliteration of **Chapter 4 ("Cyclic Groups"), §4.3 "The Method of
Repeated Squares"** of Thomas W. Judson's *Abstract Algebra: Theory and
Applications* (source: `vendor/aata/src/cyclic.xml`; © 1997–2026 Judson,
GFDL 1.3). This section is a **computational algorithm** rather than a stated
theorem, and it is the one part of Chapter 4 with no machine-checked content —
the reasons are recorded in the note at the end. The book's exposition is
reproduced here for completeness.

## The method

Computing large powers can be very time-consuming. If we want to compute
something like
$$2^{37{,}398{,}332} \pmod{46{,}389},$$
the result is a number between $0$ and $46{,}388$ that we could easily write
down — but a naive computation is infeasible. To compute powers modulo $n$
quickly we use the binary expansion of the exponent.

Any number $a$ can be written as a sum of distinct powers of $2$:
$$a = 2^{k_1} + 2^{k_2} + \cdots + 2^{k_r}, \quad k_1 < k_2 < \cdots < k_r,$$
its binary representation. For example, the binary representation of $57$ is
$111001$, since $57 = 2^0 + 2^3 + 2^4 + 2^5$.

The laws of exponents still hold in $\mathbb{Z}_n$: if $b \equiv a^x \pmod{n}$
and $c \equiv a^y \pmod{n}$, then $bc \equiv a^{x+y} \pmod{n}$. We can compute
$a^{2^k} \pmod{n}$ in $k$ multiplications by successively squaring:
$$a^{2^0},\ a^{2^1},\ a^{2^2},\ \ldots,\ a^{2^k} \pmod{n},$$
each step squaring the previous result, dividing by $n$, and keeping the
remainder.

To compute $a^m \pmod n$, expand $m$ in binary and multiply together the
$a^{2^{k_i}}$ for the bits $k_i$ that are set. For example, computing $271^{321}
\pmod{481}$: since $321 = 2^0 + 2^6 + 2^8$,
$$271^{321} \equiv 271^{2^0} \cdot 271^{2^6} \cdot 271^{2^8} \pmod{481}.$$
Repeated squaring gives $271^{2^1} \equiv 329$, $271^{2^2} \equiv 16$, and,
continuing, $271^{2^6} \equiv 419$ and $271^{2^8} \equiv 16 \pmod{481}$;
therefore
$$271^{321} \equiv 271 \cdot 419 \cdot 16 \equiv 1{,}816{,}784 \equiv 47
\pmod{481}.$$

The method of repeated squares is essential for RSA cryptography (a later
chapter), where encoding and decoding require quickly computing large powers of
integers mod $n$.
