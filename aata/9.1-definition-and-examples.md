(source: `vendor/aata/src/isomorph.xml`, lines 25–447; © 1997–2026 Judson, GFDL 1.3).
Run `2b4m check aata/9.1-definition-and-examples.md` to verify.

The isomorphism theory lives in `std/group/isomorphism.b4m`.

## Definition

**Definition.** Two groups $(G, \cdot)$ and $(H, \circ)$ are **isomorphic** if there
exists a one-to-one and onto map $\phi : G \rightarrow H$ such that the group operation
is preserved; that is,
$$\phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \circ \phi(b)$$
for all $a$ and $b$ in $G$. If $G$ is isomorphic to $H$, we write $G \cong H$. The map
$\phi$ is called an **isomorphism**.

Two groups must be *declared*, not aliased from one import: namespaces are
identity-by-origin, so `import group` twice names one theory and both sides would
collapse to the same sort. `std/group/isomorphism.b4m` therefore declares $G$ and $H$
independently and models each onto `std/group.b4m`, so their derived laws transfer
rather than being re-proved — the same forced choice `std/group/product.b4m` documents
for a direct product's two factors.

```2b4m
import isomorphism <<< "std/group/isomorphism.b4m"

// the book's two groups, their operations and identities
sort G = isomorphism.G
sort H = isomorphism.H
const eG = isomorphism.EG
const eH = isomorphism.EH
func opG = isomorphism.opG
func opH = isomorphism.opH

// φ : G → H, and ψ its inverse
func phi = isomorphism.phi
func psi = isomorphism.psi

// the three conditions that make φ an isomorphism: it preserves the operation, and the
// two round trips are identities (which is what "one-to-one and onto" buys).
axiom phiPreservesOp = isomorphism.phiPreservesOp
axiom psiAfterPhi = isomorphism.psiAfterPhi
axiom phiAfterPsi = isomorphism.phiAfterPsi
```

**A note on $\phi$'s type.** $\phi$ is a primitive function here, not an `Fn` from
`std/function.b4m`. That layer is single-sorted — `apply(f, x)` takes and returns an
`Element` — so it cannot type a map whose source and target are *different* sorts.
Bijectivity is therefore stated as the two round-trip axioms rather than borrowed as
`invertible`, and injectivity/surjectivity are derived from them below.

**Judson's examples.** $\mathbb{Z}_4 \cong \langle i \rangle$ via $\phi(n) = i^n$;
$(\mathbb{R}, +) \cong (\mathbb{R}^+, \cdot)$ via the exponential, since
$e^{x+y} = e^x e^y$; and $\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to $\{2^n\} \subset \mathbb{Q}^*$
via $\phi(n) = 2^n$. Each is a concrete map between two specific groups, and each is
checked by exhibiting the operation-preservation equation — which is what the axioms
above abstract.

## The bijection, made usable

Judson says assertions (1) and (2) of Theorem 9.6 "follow from the fact that $\phi$ is
a bijection". These are that fact in the form the later proofs need.

```2b4m
theorem phiIsInjective = isomorphism.phiIsInjective
theorem phiIsSurjective = isomorphism.phiIsSurjective
```

$\phi$ also carries $G$'s identity to $H$'s. Judson leaves this to the exercises, but
every structural claim below needs it: from $\phi(e \cdot e) = \phi(e) \circ \phi(e)$
and $e \cdot e = e$, cancellation in $H$ gives $\phi(e_G) = e_H$.

```2b4m
theorem phiPreservesIdentity = isomorphism.phiPreservesIdentity
```

## Theorem 9.6 — what an isomorphism preserves

**Theorem 9.6.** Let $\phi : G \rightarrow H$ be an isomorphism of two groups. Then the
following statements are true.

1. $\phi^{-1} : H \rightarrow G$ is an isomorphism.
2. $|G| = |H|$.
3. If $G$ is abelian, then $H$ is abelian.
4. If $G$ is cyclic, then $H$ is cyclic.
5. If $G$ has a subgroup of order $n$, then $H$ has a subgroup of order $n$.

*Proof (Judson).* Assertions (1) and (2) follow from the fact that $\phi$ is a
bijection. We will prove (3) here and leave the remainder of the theorem to be proved
in the exercises.

(3) Suppose that $h_1$ and $h_2$ are elements of $H$. Since $\phi$ is onto, there exist
elements $g_1, g_2 \in G$ such that $\phi(g_1) = h_1$ and $\phi(g_2) = h_2$. Therefore,
$$h_1 h_2 = \phi(g_1)\phi(g_2) = \phi(g_1 g_2) = \phi(g_2 g_1) = \phi(g_2)\phi(g_1) = h_2 h_1.$$
$\blacksquare$

**(1) — the inverse map is an isomorphism.** $\psi$ preserves the operation, which is
what (1) asserts beyond $\psi$ being a bijection (and that is immediate from the
round-trip axioms).

```2b4m
theorem psiPreservesOp = isomorphism.psiPreservesOp
```

**(3) — abelian transfers.** This is the assertion Judson proves in the text, and the
proof follows his chain exactly: pull $h_1$ and $h_2$ back through $\psi$, commute in
$G$, push forward through $\phi$.

```2b4m
theorem abelianTransfers = isomorphism.abelianTransfers
```

**(2), (4) and (5) are not transliterated.** (2) is $|G| = |H|$, which with counts as
*listings* (§6.2) means transporting a listing of $G$ along $\phi$ — reachable, not
built. (4) and (5) need the cyclic-subgroup and subgroup-order machinery to travel
along $\phi$, which `std/group/generated.b4m` and `std/subgroup.b4m` have over a single
group but not across an isomorphism. See `CHAPTER9-PAINPOINTS`.

## Isomorphism is an equivalence relation

**Theorem 9.10.** The isomorphism of groups determines an equivalence relation on the
class of all groups.

Judson leaves the proof as an exercise. **All three of its parts are proved**, each by
exhibiting the witness that makes the relation hold:

```2b4m
// G ≅ G: the identity map preserves the operation and is its own inverse
theorem reflexivityOfIsomorphism = isomorphism.reflexivityOfIsomorphism

// G ≅ H ⟹ H ≅ G: ψ is the witness — it preserves the operation, and the two round
// trips are the SAME axioms read with the roles swapped
theorem symmetryOfIsomorphism = isomorphism.symmetryOfIsomorphism

// G ≅ H and H ≅ K ⟹ G ≅ K: χ∘φ preserves the operation and both round trips close
// via ψ∘χ⁻¹. This needs a THIRD group, so K and a second isomorphism are declared.
theorem transitivityOfIsomorphism = isomorphism.transitivityOfIsomorphism

// and the composite is an isomorphism of GROUPS, not just a bijection preserving `op`
theorem compositePreservesIdentity = isomorphism.compositePreservesIdentity
```

What cannot be written is the **relation** $\cong$ itself. Naming it would quantify over
pairs of groups, and a group here is a *theory* — a sort plus operations plus axioms —
not a value inhabiting some sort of groups. So every instance is provable and only the
general statement "for all groups $G, H$ …" is out of reach; the mathematical content of
Theorem 9.10 is complete. See `CHAPTER9-PAINPOINTS`.

## Cayley's Theorem

**Theorem 9.12 (Cayley).** Every group is isomorphic to a group of permutations.

*Proof (Judson).* Let $G$ be a group. For any $g \in G$, define
$\lambda_g : G \rightarrow G$ by $\lambda_g(a) = ga$. We claim that $\lambda_g$ is a
permutation of $G$. To show that $\lambda_g$ is one-to-one, suppose that
$\lambda_g(a) = \lambda_g(b)$. Then $ga = gb$, hence $a = b$. To show that $\lambda_g$
is onto, we must prove that for each $a \in G$ there is a $b$ such that
$\lambda_g(b) = a$ — let $b = g^{-1}a$. $\blacksquare$

All of this is machine-checked. The **permutations of $G$** are declared as their own
sort — application, composition, and extensionality — because
`std/permutation.b4m`'s `Fn` acts on a fixed `Element` and so cannot represent
permutations of an abstract $G$. That is a limitation of that file, not of the
language: six declarations suffice, and extensionality is the load-bearing one (it
turns pointwise agreement into equality of permutations).

Judson's two claims about $\lambda_g$:

```2b4m
theorem leftTranslationIsInjective = isomorphism.leftTranslationIsInjective
theorem leftTranslationIsSurjective = isomorphism.leftTranslationIsSurjective
```

The Cayley map $g \mapsto \lambda_g$ is a **homomorphism** —
$\lambda_{gh} = \lambda_g \circ \lambda_h$, which is associativity read as a
statement about translations — and it is **injective**, so $G$ really is a copy of its
image:

```2b4m
theorem cayleyPreservesOp = isomorphism.cayleyPreservesOp
theorem cayleyIsInjective = isomorphism.cayleyIsInjective
theorem cayleyIdentity = isomorphism.cayleyIdentity
```

And the image is a **group** of permutations rather than merely a set of them: it
contains the identity and is closed under composition.

```2b4m
theorem imageHasIdentity = isomorphism.imageHasIdentity
theorem imageClosedUnderComposition = isomorphism.imageClosedUnderComposition
```

Together these are Cayley's Theorem: $G$ is isomorphic to the group
$\{\lambda_g : g \in G\}$ of permutations of $G$, via the injective
operation-preserving map $g \mapsto \lambda_g$. Closure under inverses follows from
the same argument as composition ($\lambda_g^{-1} = \lambda_{g^{-1}}$) and is left to
the reader, as Judson leaves the remaining assertions of Theorem 9.6.
